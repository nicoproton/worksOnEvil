# Introduction to Christianity

***Introduction to Christianity*** is Joseph Ratzinger's 1968 theological interpretation of the Apostles' Creed, written under the pressure of modern doubt, historical criticism, secularization, and postconciliar theological debate. It belongs in this graph because it shows Ratzinger's characteristic answer to modernity: Christian faith is not irrational escape, private feeling, or symbolic costume, but a truthful act of trust ordered toward reality, Logos, and the living God.

This is not a magisterial document. It is a theological work by a Catholic theologian who later became Benedict XVI. Its importance for the graph is that it stands between the encyclical branch and the modernity branch, translating doctrinal claims into an account of faith under modern conditions.

## Faith Under the Pressure of Unbelief

Ratzinger begins from the fact that modern believers and unbelievers are both touched by doubt. Faith is not presented as an easy possession, and unbelief is not dismissed as merely stupid or wicked.

That makes the book useful for [[Reason Ordered to Truth]]. Ratzinger's answer to modern doubt is not to abandon reason, but to show that faith itself is a rational entrustment to truth, not a blind leap into subjectivity.

## The Creed as Stable Form

The Apostles' Creed matters because it gives faith a received form. Ratzinger does not treat doctrine as a disposable shell around private religious feeling.

This makes the book a constructive neighbor of [[Dogmatic Relativism]]. It does not merely condemn relativism from outside; it shows how a stable confession can remain intelligible without being reduced to a historical artifact.

The same point keeps it near [[Theological Modernism]]. Ratzinger speaks to modern people, but he does not relocate revelation into consciousness, moral uplift, or historical progress.

## Logos and Person

Ratzinger's account of Christianity turns on the claim that ultimate reality is not blind matter, arbitrary will, or abstract law, but Logos and love. The world is intelligible because it is created by reason, and faith is personal because truth is not less than personal.

This helps distinguish [[Reason Ordered to Truth]] from mere rationalism. Reason is not reduced to calculation; it is opened toward being, meaning, personhood, and God.

## Why It Belongs Here

*Introduction to Christianity* strengthens the graph's Catholic side by giving a positive center. Many notes diagnose failed substitutes: secular providence, scientism, relativism, false salvation, and liberation detached from truth. Ratzinger shows what those substitutes are replacing: faith as truthful reception of God, not as private myth or social utility.

Its role should remain constructive. Use it where the graph needs to remember that Catholic critique is not only anti-modern rejection, but an argument that faith, reason, truth, and personhood belong together.
