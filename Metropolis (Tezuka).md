# Metropolis (Tezuka)

***Metropolis*** is Osamu Tezuka's 1949 science-fiction manga about Mitchy, an artificial humanoid created by Dr. Lawton and coveted by the Red Party for destructive political purposes. It is not an adaptation of Thea von Harbou's *[[Metropolis (Novel)]]*, though Tezuka drew inspiration from a still image of the film's robot birth. The useful connection is therefore visual and thematic rather than direct narrative inheritance.

## The Artificial Child

Mitchy is made from artificial cells and raised without knowing the truth of their origin. The central wound is not simply artificiality, but discovery: Mitchy learns that they were made as a tool and that powerful adults want to exploit them.

This places the manga beside [[Manufactured Humanity]]. Tezuka asks what happens when a made being is human enough to suffer from being treated as an instrument.

## Duke Red and the Weaponized Body

Duke Red wants to use Mitchy's powers for domination. Artificial life becomes politically valuable because it can be converted into a weapon. The problem is not creation alone, but creation captured by power.

That makes the manga a bridge between [[Technological Dystopia]] and [[Layered Conspiracy]]. The Red Party's secrecy and Duke Red's manipulation turn scientific creation into a tool for rule.

## Human, Robot, or Successor Species

The manga opens with evolutionary anxiety: advanced species may reach a point where they are replaced. Mitchy is not only an invention but a possible successor. This gives the story a sharper edge than ordinary robot fiction.

The question is close to *[[R.U.R.]]*: artificial beings are created to serve human purposes, but their existence reveals human beings' fear that they may no longer be the final form of life.

## The Lang Connection

The title and robot-birth image connect Tezuka's work to the older *Metropolis*, but the story takes its own route. Tezuka's manga is more concerned with artificial cells, identity, and power politics than with the Brain/Hands/Heart allegory of von Harbou and Lang.

Still, the two works share a motif that belongs in the graph: the made humanoid as a focus for urban modernity, class anxiety, technological wonder, and fear of replacement.
