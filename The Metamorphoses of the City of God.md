# The Metamorphoses of the City of God

***The Metamorphoses of the City of God*** is Etienne Gilson's 1952 study of the afterlives and transformations of Augustine's City of God in Western thought. It belongs in this graph because it gives a precise historical-philosophical bridge between Christian historical hope and later secular projects that try to build salvation inside history.

## Augustine as the Original Pattern

Gilson begins from the Augustinian problem: history has meaning, but the City of God is not identical with any earthly political order. The heavenly city is present in pilgrimage and conflict, but it is not the product of empire, nation, class, science, or administration.

This makes the book central to [[Secularized City of God]]. Later thinkers can inherit the shape of Christian history while changing its center.

## The City Becomes Immanent

The graph-useful movement is the transformation of Christian eschatology into worldly projects. Once the city is relocated into history itself, political and cultural systems can begin to promise what belongs to divine salvation.

This connects the work to [[Progress as Secular Providence]]. The future becomes judge, redeemer, and horizon of meaning after providence has been translated into historical development.

## Against Political Salvation

The book also clarifies a boundary for [[Christian Civil Order]]. Catholic political thought can affirm civil order, law, authority, and common good without identifying any earthly regime with the City of God.

This matters because the graph contains many false unities: world state, revolutionary humanity, scientific planning, secular religion, and humanitarian empire. Gilson gives a historical way to see them as metamorphoses of an older Christian pattern.

## Why It Belongs Here

*The Metamorphoses of the City of God* is the most graph-expansive Gilson work. It links Augustine, progress, secular providence, political religion, and false salvation without flattening them into the same thing. It helps explain how Christian ideas can survive as forms after their theological center has been displaced.
