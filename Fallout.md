# Fallout

***Fallout*** is the 1997 post-apocalyptic role-playing game set in a retrofuturist American wasteland after nuclear war. Its strongest relevance to this graph is not the general wasteland atmosphere, but the survival of pre-war military science as a sterile power that continues to define humanity as material for experiment, mutation, purification, or replacement.

## The Old World Did Not Repent

The world before the bombs possessed extraordinary science, industry, weapons, robotics, vault technology, and military research. Its fall is therefore not only material collapse. It is a moral judgment on a civilization whose technical power outran wisdom.

This makes the game a strong case of [[Fallen High Civilization]]. The ruins are full of old-world systems that still function, but what they preserve is often not memory or prudence. They preserve command structures, laboratories, weapons, and experiments.

## West Tek, Mariposa, and FEV

The Forced Evolutionary Virus begins as pre-war military biotechnology. Mariposa Military Base is the key location where the old scientific order becomes visible as a secret program of human alteration.

This places the game near [[Sterile Scientific Salvation]]. Science remains powerful after catastrophe, but its surviving forms are sealed facilities, military protocols, and biological experiments that treat human bodies as material for a future design.

## Vault-Tec and the Experimental Ark

The vault system gives the series its most ironic image of secular salvation. A vault should be an ark: an enclosure that preserves a remnant through catastrophe so that human life can continue. In the broader early-series lore, many vaults become experimental worlds instead. Their inhabitants are not only protected from the bombs; they are sorted, isolated, observed, stressed, exposed, or preserved for later use.

This makes the vault one of the graph's clearest secular forms of [[Sealed Refuge]]. Shelter is real, but the enclosure gives the institution total access to the people it claims to preserve.

This is a precise form of [[Managed Crisis]]. Nuclear war creates the justification for total enclosure, and total enclosure gives the experimenter complete access to human life. The crisis does not merely threaten the population; it makes the population governable as data.

The first game handles this more quietly than later franchise lore. Vault 13 is important because it can still be read as a genuine shelter, while Vault 12 and Necropolis already show the horror of a vault that fails, or is allowed to fail, into radiation exposure. *Fallout 2* then sharpens the implication by making vault populations valuable to the Enclave as controlled samples of humanity.

The vault idea therefore belongs near [[Closed World of Absolute License]] in a technocratic rather than libertine form. Inside the sealed world, ordinary public witness is gone. The institution can call itself preservation while treating residents as experimental material.

## The Master and Unity

The Master is the central reason *Fallout* belongs in this branch. His Unity is not simple sadism. It is a salvation program: end human weakness, radiation damage, tribal conflict, and future war by transforming humanity into super mutants.

That makes the Master a strong case of [[Designed Successor Humanity]] and [[Forced Human Completion]]. His cure abolishes the very humanity it claims to rescue. The horror is not only mutation, but compulsory completion according to a system's idea of what humanity must become.

## The False Cure of Forced Evolution

Unity answers real wounds. The wasteland is violent, irradiated, divided, and fragile. The Master sees those wounds and proposes a biological solution.

This is [[False Cure]] in post-nuclear form. The wound is real, but the cure becomes domination because it removes consent, inheritance, ordinary generation, and the right of persons to remain themselves.

## The Brotherhood of Steel and Guarded Preservation

The Brotherhood of Steel offers a different response to the same old-world catastrophe. Its origin is tied to revulsion against Mariposa's FEV experiments: soldiers discover what scientific authority has been doing to human subjects and break with the chain of command.

This makes the Brotherhood a partial counter-force to [[Sterile Scientific Salvation]]. It recognizes that technology without moral restraint can become monstrous. But its answer is not full repentance or reconstruction. It becomes preservation, quarantine, hierarchy, and guarded custody of dangerous knowledge.

That places the Brotherhood near [[Lost Technology as Temptation]]. Its fear is not foolish: old weapons and research can repeat the old fall. The danger is that stewardship of technology can harden into hoarding, ritualized rank, and a sterile identity built around possession of the very relics it fears.

## Vault 13 and the Difference Between Shelter and System

Vault 13 matters because it begins as a shelter rather than a revealed laboratory of domination. The Vault Dweller leaves a protected enclosure and discovers that survival by enclosure is not enough. The old world outside has left behind powers that still claim authority over the future.

This distinguishes the first game's strongest pattern from the later general "vault experiment" trope. The core issue is not merely that a pleasant front hides evil. It is that postwar humanity is surrounded by old scientific systems still trying to solve the human problem.

## Fallout 2 and the Enclave

*Fallout 2* sharpens the pattern through the Enclave. Where the Master wants to transform everyone into a new humanity, the Enclave narrows humanity to itself and treats the irradiated world as contamination.

This makes the Enclave a strong case of [[Purification by Exclusion]]. Its eugenic logic is different from Unity's forced completion: the Master says old humanity must be converted; the Enclave says most existing humans are no longer really the humanity worth saving.

The Enclave also belongs to [[Sterile Scientific Salvation]]. It is the old military-political-scientific order surviving as a bunker remnant, still imagining itself as the legitimate future after its civilization has already destroyed the world.

## Vault City and Sterile Civic Success

*Fallout 2* also gives a smaller but useful variation in Vault City. Unlike the Master's vats or the Enclave's genocide plan, Vault City is a functioning civic order produced from vault success. Its danger is not chaos, but sterile superiority.

That makes Vault City a civic form of [[Hollowed Order]]. Law, medicine, cleanliness, citizenship, and technical competence remain, but they are bound to exclusion, hierarchy, and disdain for wasteland life. The city shows that the vault can succeed materially while still producing a narrowed account of who counts.

This is not as central as the Master or Enclave, but it is a good reminder that the series does not only fear mad scientists. It also fears humane-looking order when order is separated from mercy.

## Fallout Tactics and the Calculator

*Fallout Tactics* is secondary, but its Calculator and Vault 0 belong to the same pattern. The old world attempts to preserve command intelligence through brains, computers, vault infrastructure, and robotics, then hands the postwar future to an automated pacification system.

This is close to the machine-god branch and to Wasteland's automated postwar control pattern. It belongs near [[Machine as God]] and [[Sterile Scientific Salvation]] because the machine claims the role of postwar coordinator after the human order has collapsed.

The Calculator is not as central to the first game's moral structure as the Master, but it clarifies the series' recurring alternatives: mutate humanity, purify humanity, lock humanity in vaults, or pacify humanity by machine.

## New Vegas: House and Big MT

*Fallout: New Vegas* is uneven for this branch, but two elements are useful. Mr. House is the old-world planner who survives by technological preservation and tries to restart civilization through calculation, securitrons, capital, and personal sovereignty. He is less an amoral scientist than a preserved technocratic will.

House therefore belongs only lightly near [[Sterile Scientific Salvation]]. His project is not eugenic replacement, but the continuation of old-world command through one preserved mind.

Big MT is stronger. The Think Tank turns science into comic grotesque: brains in jars, lobotomites, cybernetic mutilation, animal experimentation, personality loops, and research continuing after every humane purpose has disappeared. Its comedy matters because it exposes the absurdity inside sterile science. The laboratory keeps producing results long after it has forgotten why anyone should want them.

Big MT is one of the clearest later Fallout examples of [[Weaponized Rationalism]]. It is not rational in the deeper sense; it is intelligence trapped in procedure, curiosity, and technical manipulation after wisdom has died.

## Literary and Film Ancestors

The game's wasteland surface has a clear neighbor in *Mad Max 2*: desert roads, raiders, scavenged machines, fortified settlements, and the collapse of industrial civilization into fuel, violence, and barter. That connection is strongest at the level of iconography and tone.

The deeper bunker branch now belongs more cleanly under [[Sealed Refuge]], while the laboratory and post-nuclear body branch has a literary neighbor in *Dr. Bloodmoney*: bodily alteration, guilt, and damaged community rather than clean heroic rebuilding.

## Why It Belongs Here

*Fallout* belongs because it gives the graph the American Cold War satirical form of sterile scientific salvation. Nuclear war destroys the old world, but the old world's laboratories and survival projects keep asking how humanity should be remade, purified, or replaced.
