# Subsidiarity

Subsidiarity names the Catholic social principle that higher and larger forms of authority should support, coordinate, and protect lower and smaller societies rather than absorb their proper functions. The state is real and necessary, but family, local association, guild, profession, municipality, Church, and other intermediate bodies also have real social life.

This bridge belongs under [[Economic Order Under Moral Law]] and [[Christian Civil Order]]. It is useful when the question is not whether authority should exist, but at what level a task belongs, and whether help from above becomes domination.

Use this link when:

- the state or central system absorbs functions that belong to families, local communities, associations, or professions;
- a larger authority is needed to protect the weak but must not replace the lower society it helps;
- social order is rebuilt through intermediate bodies rather than isolated individuals facing an all-powerful state;
- central planning, economic monopoly, or bureaucratic administration weakens smaller forms of social life.

*[[Quadragesimo Anno]]* is the main encyclical anchor. Pius XI gives the classic formulation: what smaller and lower communities can do should not be transferred to a larger and higher community, though higher authority may intervene to help, coordinate, and restore order.

*[[Rerum Novarum]]* gives the precursor through family, worker associations, mutual aid, and the rejection of state absorption. Leo XIII does not use the later formula in the same way, but he already defends the social bodies that subsidiarity names more explicitly.

*[[Seeing Like a State]]* is a secular political-theory neighbor rather than a Catholic source. Scott's critique of high-modernist schemes helps show why larger systems fail when they absorb or ignore local practical knowledge, intermediate bodies, and correction from below.

This bridge is a counterweight to [[Life Redesigned by Ideology]] and [[Administered Human Formation]]. It does not reject order or reform. It rejects the fantasy that social life becomes healthier when every function is drawn upward into a single planner, expert class, market power, or state machine.
