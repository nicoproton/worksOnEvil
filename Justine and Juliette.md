# Justine and Juliette

***Justine*** and ***Juliette*** are Sade's paired narratives of virtue punished and vice rewarded. They are useful as a single graph node because their philosophical force lies in contrast: Justine suffers by remaining virtuous, while Juliette prospers by embracing evil.

## Virtue Punished, Vice Rewarded

The strongest concept here is [[Inverted Providence]]. Sade does not merely write a tragic world where the innocent suffer. He repeatedly imagines a world whose structure appears to teach that virtue is ignorance and vice is wisdom.

That makes the works important beside [[Providential Counter-Revolution]] by contrast. De Maistre sees revolutionary suffering as judgment inside providence. Sade imagines the more radical negation: suffering as proof that moral providence is a lie.

## Evil as Doctrine

The libertines who surround Justine and Juliette are not only villains of appetite. They often explain themselves. Their crimes come with speeches about nature, power, pleasure, religion, and the uselessness of pity.

This places the works under [[Libertine Rationalism]]. Sadean evil is especially relevant to the graph because it turns transgression into theory. It helps explain the ideological background of later systems that treat the person as material once moral limits are dismissed.

## Against Privatio Boni

These works should not be linked to [[Privatio Boni]] as if they naturally teach it. Their native pressure is closer to anti-providence or even a dark inversion of providence: evil appears active, rewarded, and aligned with nature.

They can still be read in argument with privation. From a Christian reading, Sade's world is horrifying precisely because it mistakes parasitic appetite for fullness. But that is an interpretive conflict, not the work's own metaphysics.

## Why It Belongs Here

*Justine* and *Juliette* belong because they expose a philosophical current behind modern anti-order: not merely sin enjoyed, but sin explained as truth. The graph can use them to distinguish ordinary decadence from a more systematic anti-Christian anthropology.
