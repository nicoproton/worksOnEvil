# The Age of Secularization

***The Age of Secularization*** is Augusto Del Noce's collection of essays on postwar secularization, Marxism, progressivism, scientism, the sexual revolution, and the affluent society. It belongs in this graph because it explains how revolutionary negation can survive after revolution itself has lost its messianic force.

This is the Del Noce work most directly useful for the vault's later modern branch. It helps connect Catholic critiques of atheism and progress to consumer society, technocratic culture, sexual liberation, and nihilism.

## Marxism After Its Political Failure

Del Noce's striking claim is that Marxism can fail in the East while realizing itself in the West. This does not mean that the West becomes formally communist. It means that Marxism's negations can be absorbed by the affluent society: rejection of transcendence, rejection of permanent values, and reduction of man to material-historical conditions.

This gives the book a strong relation to [[Atheistic Communism]], but it should not be collapsed into that concept. The encyclical condemnation of communism concerns an anti-religious collectivist system. Del Noce asks what happens after the revolutionary system loses force but its metaphysical denials remain culturally active.

## The Prosperous Form of Nihilism

The most important bridge is [[Affluent Nihilism]]. Del Noce is useful because he sees that nihilism need not appear as despair, poverty, or terror. It can appear as comfort, permissiveness, consumption, therapeutic adaptation, and progress without truth.

In this sense, the affluent society is not the opposite of revolutionary negation. It may be the place where negation becomes ordinary life. A society can keep the comforts of bourgeois life while accepting the anti-metaphysical conclusions of revolution.

## Scientism and Relativism

Del Noce links the postwar progressive mentality to scientism: the belief that empirical science is the only serious rationality. But if science is treated as the only form of knowledge, it cannot provide final ends. The result is a strange combination: technical confidence joined to moral relativism.

This connects the book to [[Weaponized Rationalism]]. The problem is not science itself, but reason reduced to method, measurement, and power while losing access to truth, wisdom, and finality.

It also deepens [[Dogmatic Relativism]] at a cultural distance. In the theological branch, doctrine is made answerable to historical fashion. In Del Noce's secular branch, all stable truth claims become suspect because history and science have been turned into judges over metaphysics.

## Sexual Revolution and Liberation from Order

Del Noce treats sexual revolution not as a separate moral panic but as part of the same metaphysical shift. If there is no truth about human nature beyond empirical and social construction, then liberation appears as release from inherited moral limits.

This gives the book a strong relation to [[Liberty Detached from Truth]]. Freedom becomes the removal of repression rather than the free pursuit of the good.

The connection also helps explain the darker aesthetic branch. Sadean libertinism, decadent self-enclosure, Ballardian technological desire, and commodity-surface satire are not all saying Del Noce's argument, but they show worlds in which liberation from moral order does not necessarily produce deeper humanity.

## Why It Belongs Here

*The Age of Secularization* is one of the best Catholic philosophical bridges between the encyclical branch and the late-modern fiction branch. It explains why consumerism, technocracy, relativism, sexual liberation, and nihilism belong in the same conversation without reducing them to a simple conspiracy.

Its value is not that it condemns modernity from outside. Del Noce tries to read modernity as a history of philosophical choices. That makes the note useful for the graph's method: relationships should be discovered through intelligible patterns, not imposed by prejudice.
