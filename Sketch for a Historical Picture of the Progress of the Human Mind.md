# Sketch for a Historical Picture of the Progress of the Human Mind

***Sketch for a Historical Picture of the Progress of the Human Mind*** is the final major work of the Marquis de Condorcet, written in 1794 while he was in hiding during the French Revolution and published posthumously in 1795. It is one of the clearest Enlightenment statements of historical progress: human beings, through reason, science, education, and political reform, can continue improving knowledge, liberty, equality, and welfare.

## Progress as Historical Hope

Condorcet reads history as a long struggle against ignorance, tyranny, superstition, inequality, and arbitrary power. His confidence is not merely technological. It includes moral and political improvement: the spread of rights, the decline of prejudice, the abolition of slavery, and the extension of education.

This makes the book a central anchor for [[Progress as Secular Providence]]. The future functions almost providentially: it reveals the meaning of the past and offers hope that reason can overcome inherited oppression.

The link should remain careful. Condorcet is not writing a dystopia. The work contains genuine humane aspirations, especially around equality and freedom. Its importance for the graph is that the same grammar of progress can later become a basis for managed human development.

## Science, Education, and Human Betterment

Condorcet's future depends on the expansion of knowledge. Science is not only a body of discoveries; it is a model for human improvement because it can correct error, expose prejudice, and make social life more rational.

This gives the work an ancestral relation to [[Scientific World State]] without making Condorcet a simple technocrat. He imagines reason spreading through education and public reform more than through a single administrative world-machine.

The danger for the graph appears when education and reform become [[Administered Human Formation]]. Condorcet's hope is that people become freer through enlightenment; later systems can turn the same formative ambition into adaptation to a planned order.

## Revolution and Ambiguity

The book's historical situation matters. Condorcet writes under revolutionary danger, and the optimism of the work is sharpened by catastrophe around him. The promise of progress is not naive comfort; it is written from inside political failure, persecution, and death.

That makes the note useful beside darker Revolution nodes. *[[The Gods Are Athirst]]* shows revolutionary virtue becoming Terror. *[[Considerations on France]]* reads the same upheaval as providential judgment. Condorcet gives the opposite pole: history may still be intelligible as emancipation.

## Why It Belongs Here

The *Sketch* belongs in the graph because it gives the Revolution branch its strongest language of hopeful progress. Without it, the graph would overrepresent conspiracy, terror, and counter-revolution. Condorcet shows why progress became morally compelling before it became suspicious.
