# Unite d'Habitation, Marseille

The Unite d'Habitation in Marseille, also known as the Cite Radieuse, is Le Corbusier's large collective housing block commissioned after the Second World War and completed in 1952. It belongs in the graph because it is the built hinge between Le Corbusier's earlier machine-age housing theory, his urban doctrine, and the later Brutalist turn toward raw concrete, collective infrastructure, and institutional mass.

It is not a book about Brutalism. It is one of the buildings through which Brutalism becomes imaginable.

## Source Base

This note reads the Unite d'Habitation as a built work through the building itself, Fondation Le Corbusier material, the Le Corbusier World Heritage framing, UNESCO's account of Le Corbusier's architectural work, and later architectural history of Brutalism. The building should not be treated as a manifesto speaking in one voice. Its meaning comes from commission, plan, construction, material, use, reception, and afterlife.

## Postwar Housing and the Vertical City

The building emerges from the emergency of postwar reconstruction and rehousing. Le Corbusier's answer is not a traditional apartment block, a garden suburb, or a low-rise planned neighborhood. It is a "vertical city": a large residential block raised on pilotis, containing apartments, internal streets, commercial and shared facilities, and a roofscape for communal use.

This makes the building a major built case of [[Machine for Living]]. The house-machine has become collective dwelling: apartment, corridor, shop, roof, sun, air, structure, circulation, and shared service are arranged as one apparatus for modern domestic life.

The promise is serious. The building tries to combine density with light, privacy with collective facilities, and urban living with air and open surroundings.

## Individual and Collective

The building's central social question is the balance between individual dwelling and collective life. Apartments are private units, but the whole block is conceived as a shared organism with internal streets, services, rooftop amenities, and a larger social identity.

This places the building near [[Administered Human Formation]], though not as a dystopian case. It is an environment meant to form modern domestic life by arranging the conditions under which families live, shop, walk, gather, and use public facilities.

The tension is that collective provision can protect the household from chaos, but it can also pre-design the terms of common life before a community has grown through custom, memory, parish, street, market, or local inheritance.

## Raw Concrete and the Brutalist Afterlife

The Unite is one of the key sources for the later Brutalist imagination because of its use of rough reinforced concrete, board-marked surfaces, massive form, and visible material presence. Its concrete is not merely a neutral material choice. It gives the building a public body: heavy, tactile, imperfect, and difficult to assimilate into polite modernist smoothness.

This is the strongest connection to Banham's *[[The New Brutalism - Ethic or Aesthetic]]*. Banham's question appears here in built form. Is the roughness an ethic of material truth, construction, economy, and collective seriousness? Or does it become an aesthetic of raw power?

The answer should remain open. The Unite is both humane and severe, inventive and controlling, social and monumental.

## Modulor, Body, and Measure

The building is also tied to Le Corbusier's Modulor system, his attempt to relate architectural measurement to the human body through proportion. This matters because the Unite is not simply a block of concrete imposed on anonymous units. It is meant to join industrial repetition with bodily measure.

That gives the building a complicated relation to [[Embodied Relation]]. The Modulor tries to keep architecture tied to human scale, but the total block still risks making the body a measured input inside a larger designed apparatus.

This ambiguity is central to Le Corbusier's late work. He does not abandon the human body; he abstracts and regulates it.

## From Radiant City to Built Block

The Unite should be read beside *[[The Radiant City]]*, but not as a literal city plan. It is a single block that carries pieces of the radiant-city idea: sun, air, collective housing, services, standardization, greenery, vertical organization, and the attempt to reconcile individual life with planned collective provision.

It also clarifies why Le Corbusier's influence over later architecture is not only urban and diagrammatic. Even when his plans for whole cities were not built, the collective housing block became a portable form.

That portability matters for Brutalism. Later architects could take from the Unite its raw concrete, deck or street-in-the-air logic, collective facilities, public mass, and social ambition without taking all of Le Corbusier's urban doctrine.

## Why It Belongs Here

The Unite d'Habitation belongs because it lets the vault connect Le Corbusier's theory to a built object that shaped postwar architectural imagination. It is the place where the house-machine, collective housing, raw concrete, public provision, and Brutalist afterlife meet. It is not simply an example of bad planning or heroic modernism; it is a hinge where humane social ambition and administered concrete monumentality become hard to separate.
