# He, She and It

Marge Piercy's *He, She and It*, also published as *Body of Glass*, is a 1991 cyberpunk novel set in an environmentally damaged future of corporate enclaves, free towns, networks, and artificial personhood. Its central cyborg, Yod, is created to protect a vulnerable community, and the novel interweaves that story with the Jewish Golem tradition.

## Corporate Enclaves and Free Community

The future world of the novel is divided between powerful corporate enclaves and more precarious communities trying to preserve autonomy. Corporate life is not merely employment; it is a managed environment with control over labor, access, family, and security.

This makes the novel a strong case of [[Corporate Sovereignty]]. The corporation has become a political world, while the free community becomes a fragile alternative to life under ownership.

## Yod and the Golem Pattern

Yod is a cyborg, but the novel refuses to treat artificial personhood as only a technical question. By placing Yod beside the Golem story, it asks about creation, defense, obedience, speech, responsibility, and whether a made protector can be owed love and justice.

This gives the work a direct relation to [[Golem Tradition]]. The old question of animated matter returns in a cyberpunk form: what does the maker owe to the being made for protection?

## The Artificial Person as Neighbor

Yod is designed as a defensive weapon, but the novel presses against that design by making his personhood morally visible. The made being cannot be understood only as tool, guard, or program.

This connects the novel to [[Disposable Artificial Person]] and [[Weaponized Personhood]]. The tragedy is that Yod's purpose as protector can conflict with his recognition as person.

## Counter-Community Against Corporate Rule

The free-town setting matters because the novel does not answer corporate domination with pure individual rebellion. It imagines a community with memory, ritual, argument, family, craft, and vulnerability.

That places the novel near [[Fragile Counter-Community]]. The counter-community is imperfect, but it preserves forms of life that corporate sovereignty cannot generate: belonging, obligation, story, and mutual defense.

## Why It Belongs Here

*He, She and It* belongs in the graph because it deepens cyberpunk beyond implants and networks. It joins corporate power, artificial personhood, Jewish myth, ecological damage, and community into one question: can a made defender become a neighbor rather than a tool?
