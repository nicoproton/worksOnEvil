# Today and Tomorrow

***Today and Tomorrow*** is Henry Ford's 1926 statement of industrial production, standardization, flow, waste reduction, and the social meaning of mass manufacture. It belongs in this graph because Fordism is one of the major non-architectural sources for the modernist dream of standardized form serving ordinary life.

Ford's importance is not that modern architects simply copied factories. It is that mass production made a new moral imagination available: standardization could appear democratic, efficient, rational, and socially progressive.

## Production as Social Order

Ford treats production as more than business technique. Properly organized industry promises lower prices, better goods, higher wages, less waste, and broad material uplift.

This places the book under [[Life Redesigned by Ideology]]. Industrial method becomes a social philosophy: the world can be improved by disciplined production, standardization, coordination, and elimination of waste.

## Standardization and Type

The standardized product matters deeply for modern architecture. Le Corbusier's admiration for ships, airplanes, automobiles, and standard types belongs to the same machine-age atmosphere.

This connects the work to [[Machine for Living]]. The standardized house, efficient apartment, rational kitchen, and repeatable housing block become thinkable because industrial production has made the type morally and economically attractive.

## Fordism Across Regimes

Fordism also helps explain why similar design logics could travel across hostile political systems. American capitalism, Soviet industrialization, European modernism, and technocratic planning could all admire the same features: flow, standard parts, discipline, coordination, output, and efficiency.

This does not mean they shared one politics. It means industrial method could become a common language across ideological enemies.

## Why It Belongs Here

*Today and Tomorrow* belongs because it gives the graph a key industrial bridge between capitalism, modernist design, and social planning. It helps explain why architecture could begin to imagine houses, objects, and cities as standardized instruments for a better future.
