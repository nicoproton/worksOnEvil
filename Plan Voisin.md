# Plan Voisin

*Plan Voisin* is Le Corbusier's 1925 proposal for redeveloping central Paris, presented in connection with the Pavillon de l'Esprit Nouveau and the planning ideas developed in *[[The City of Tomorrow and Its Planning]]*. The project proposed replacing large areas of the existing right-bank urban fabric with a radically ordered composition of cruciform high-rise towers, broad traffic arteries, open ground, and greenery.

The plan belongs in the graph because it makes architectural modernism's promise and danger visible at urban scale. It is not only an image of towers. It is a diagram of how the inherited city can be seen as obsolete material awaiting rational replacement.

The name is itself revealing. Voisin was associated with the aircraft and automobile world that helped finance the Pavillon de l'Esprit Nouveau. The project therefore does not only use machine-age forms as metaphors; it appears inside a cultural alliance between modern planning, publicity, industry, transportation, and the architect as public reformer.

## Source Base

This note reads Plan Voisin as an unbuilt proposal, exhibition project, and project-image tied to Le Corbusier's urban doctrine. It is not treating Paris itself as the speaking work. The evidence is the plan, its presentation context, Le Corbusier's own explanations, and later architectural history of the proposal.

## Paris as Replaceable Fabric

The most important feature of Plan Voisin is not any single tower form. It is the treatment of existing Paris as something that can be cut away for a clearer order. Dense streets, mixed uses, irregular blocks, and historical accumulation appear as congestion, unhygienic confusion, and resistance to modern circulation.

This makes the plan one of the clearest cases of [[High-Modernist Planning]]. The old city is not repaired by small accretions or local knowledge. It is redrawn from above according to legibility, geometry, traffic, light, air, and functional order.

The violence here is conceptual before it is physical. A lived city becomes a planning surface.

## Towers in Open Space

The cruciform high-rise towers are meant to concentrate density while freeing the ground. The plan promises air, sunlight, greenery, and modern hygiene rather than the dark congestion of older streets.

This connects the project to [[Machine for Living]]. The city is treated as an apparatus for healthier and more efficient life: tower, park, road, office, and dwelling become coordinated parts of a planned mechanism.

The promise should be taken seriously. Le Corbusier is not merely proposing ugliness for its own sake. He is responding to real concerns about congestion, traffic, bad housing, disease, and urban inefficiency. The graph question is whether the cure treats the city as a living inheritance or as a technical object to be purified.

## Traffic Arteries and the Rule of Circulation

Plan Voisin gives traffic a governing role. Broad axes and separated flows cut through the city, making circulation legible and fast. Movement becomes a primary ordering principle.

This places the project near [[Domination Through the Machine]]. The automobile and the traffic artery do not merely serve urban life; they help define what urban order should be. The city is reconstructed around flow.

This is one of the places where the machine logic becomes spatial. A street is no longer primarily a public room, market edge, neighborhood place, or site of chance encounter. It becomes an artery for circulation.

## Anti-Inheritance and Historical Memory

The plan is so important because it proposes modern order not on empty land but against one of Europe's most symbolically dense cities. Paris is a historical, literary, political, religious, commercial, and urban memory-field. Plan Voisin imagines that much of this accumulated texture can be replaced by a purified modern system.

This gives the project a strong contrast with [[Embodied Relation]] and with *[[The Nature of Gothic]]*. Ruskin's Gothic values irregularity, craft, spiritual labor, and historical depth. Plan Voisin values clarity, standardization, visibility, circulation, and geometric command.

The contrast is not merely stylistic. It is two accounts of what a city is: an inherited body of human relations, or an instrument to be made rational.

## False Salvation Through Spatial Purification

Plan Voisin also belongs near [[False Salvation Through Systems]], but carefully. Its promised goods are intelligible: sunlight, fresh air, open ground, traffic order, clean housing, and release from congestion.

The false-salvation danger appears when these goods are gathered into a single system that treats human disorder as solvable by demolition and reconstruction. The city is purified by removing the very mixtures through which urban life often becomes human: old streets, small trades, memory, friction, neighborhood improvisation, and unplanned encounter.

## The Project That Did Not Happen

Plan Voisin was not built. That matters. Its power is partly symbolic: it survives as an image of planning desire, a thought-experiment in urban erasure, and a warning about the dream of the fully legible city.

Because it remained unbuilt, it should not be blamed directly for every later modernist housing failure. It may also have been partly polemical: a shock-image by which Le Corbusier made himself legible as the architect who could answer the modern metropolis. But even as polemic it crystallizes a logic that became influential: towers in open space, separation of functions, traffic hierarchy, and the preference for abstract urban order over inherited street fabric.

## Why It Belongs Here

*Plan Voisin* belongs in the graph because it is the clearest project-image of the city as machine for living. It shows how humane motives such as health and light can merge with top-down abstraction, anti-inheritance, and technical command. The result is one of the graph's strongest non-fiction examples of the planned city as a spiritual and social problem.
