# The Crisis of Modernity

***The Crisis of Modernity*** is an English selection of Augusto Del Noce's essays on modern philosophy, atheism, revolution, secularization, authority, freedom, Marxism, fascism, and the affluent society. It belongs in this graph as a compact synthetic Del Noce node: the crisis of modernity is not one ideology alone, but the movement by which reason, freedom, politics, science, and culture are detached from metaphysical truth.

## Modernity as a Philosophical Drama

Del Noce does not treat modern history as a neutral sequence of events. He reads it as the unfolding of philosophical options. Rationalism, immanentism, scientism, Marxism, fascism, and postwar progressivism are not identical, but they are connected by decisions about God, man, history, and reason.

This places the book near [[Progress as Secular Providence]]. Modernity often tells its own story as emancipation: from theology, hierarchy, tradition, metaphysics, repression, and inherited authority. Del Noce asks whether this story liberates man or prepares a smaller and more controllable image of him.

## Revolution, Fascism, and Secularization

Del Noce's analysis of modern ideologies resists a simple left-right map. Fascism and communism are not merely enemies; they can both be read as moments inside a wider secularization of politics, where historical action takes on a salvific or absolute role.

That connects the book to [[Political Idolatry]]. The state, party, race, class, leader, or historical process can become a substitute absolute.

It also touches [[Atheistic Communism]], especially where Marxism becomes a theory of man, history, and redemption without God. Del Noce's added value is to ask what happens when communism's revolutionary religion collapses into postwar nihilism.

## Authority Versus Power

One of Del Noce's most useful distinctions for this graph is between authority and power. In the classical and Christian sense, authority can liberate because it orders the person toward truth and the good. In modern progressive imagination, authority is often reduced to repression, while power disguises itself as liberation.

This gives the book a strong relation to [[Liberty Detached from Truth]]. False liberty rejects authority as such, but may end by submitting the person to social pressure, technical systems, propaganda, appetite, and state or market power.

It also supports [[Reason Ordered to Truth]]. Authority is not merely force when it is rooted in evidence, truth, and reality. The mind can be freed by what it did not invent.

## Scientism as Total Culture

Del Noce's critique of scientism helps tie the Catholic branch to the technocratic branch. If empirical science is treated as the whole of reason, then non-empirical truth is excluded in advance. Yet science by itself cannot provide the ends of life.

This connects to [[Weaponized Rationalism]]. Modern reason becomes dangerous not by being too rational, but by shrinking reason until only technique, measurement, and control remain.

It also clarifies why *[[The Abolition of Man]]* is so important in the graph. Lewis's Conditioners and Del Noce's scientistic modernity are not the same argument, but both warn that a culture which denies objective value will not become neutral. It will become available to conditioning.

## Why It Belongs Here

*The Crisis of Modernity* is useful as a hub for Del Noce's overall contribution. *[[The Problem of Atheism]]* gives the philosophical root. *[[The Age of Secularization]]* gives the postwar cultural form. *The Crisis of Modernity* gathers the pattern: a culture that promises emancipation from metaphysics may end with power, technique, relativism, and practical nihilism.

This note should remain a diagnostic bridge, not a master key. Del Noce helps explain many nodes in the graph, but he should not flatten all modern works into one story.
