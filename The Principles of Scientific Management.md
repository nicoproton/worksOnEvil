# The Principles of Scientific Management

***The Principles of Scientific Management*** is Frederick Winslow Taylor's 1911 statement of scientific management. It belongs in this graph because it gives the industrial-method version of a wider modernist claim: work can be analyzed, standardized, timed, optimized, and reorganized by expert knowledge.

Taylor is not writing about architecture, but his method helps explain why twentieth-century design and planning so often imagine life as a process to be measured and improved.

## Expert Analysis of Work

Taylor's central move is to remove work from inherited habit, worker discretion, and rule of thumb. Each task should be studied, broken down, timed, and reorganized according to the one best method.

This makes the book a key industrial case of [[Life Redesigned by Ideology]]. The doctrine is not merely "work harder." It is that knowledge belongs to the system, and the system can redesign the worker's motions.

## From Workshop to Environment

The method travels beyond the factory. Once work can be scientifically decomposed and recomposed, the same logic can be applied to kitchens, apartments, schools, offices, traffic, housing, and cities.

This connects the book to [[Machine for Living]]. The house-machine and functional city become more plausible in a world where human action is already being treated as a sequence of optimizable tasks.

## Management and Formation

Scientific management also belongs near [[Administered Human Formation]]. It trains workers for a designed process. The worker is not only commanded, but fitted to a system whose knowledge is held by managers, engineers, and planners.

The promise is efficiency, higher productivity, less waste, and sometimes higher wages. The danger is that embodied skill and practical judgment are transferred from person to system.

## Why It Belongs Here

*The Principles of Scientific Management* belongs because it gives the graph a non-aesthetic source for modern design logic. It shows how the same faith in analysis, standardization, and expert control could enter factory work, architecture, planning, education, and social organization.
