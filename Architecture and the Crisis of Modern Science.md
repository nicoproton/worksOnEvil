# Architecture and the Crisis of Modern Science

***Architecture and the Crisis of Modern Science*** is Alberto Perez-Gomez's 1983 study of how architectural theory was transformed by the scientific revolution and by the rise of technical, functional, and representational ways of thinking. Its central value for this graph is diagnostic: it asks how architecture came to understand itself less as a symbolic, poetic, and cosmological art, and more as a technical discipline of measurement, mechanics, representation, and control.

The book belongs here because it reaches behind [[Machine for Living]] and [[High-Modernist Planning]]. Le Corbusier, Bauhaus functionalism, and Soviet Constructivism do not appear from nowhere. Perez-Gomez traces an older intellectual shift in which number, geometry, drawing, and scientific method increasingly lose their symbolic and metaphysical weight and become instruments for technical production.

## Number and Geometry Before Reduction

The book begins from the older role of number, proportion, and geometry in architectural thought. These were not merely tools for making buildings stand up or for drawing them accurately. They carried claims about cosmic order, harmony, beauty, and the relation between human making and the structure of reality.

This gives the book a deep relation to [[Architecture as Public Language]]. Architecture once claimed to speak through proportion, order, ornament, material, and cosmological analogy. It did not only solve practical problems; it offered a world made intelligible in built form.

The point should be handled carefully. Perez-Gomez is not asking the graph to accept every premodern numerological or magical claim as true. The important point is historical and philosophical: architectural meaning was once tied to a larger symbolic order that modern technical reason gradually made unintelligible.

## The Scientific Revolution Enters Architecture

The book follows the transformation of architectural theory from the late Renaissance into the eighteenth and early nineteenth centuries. Claude Perrault's challenge to inherited ideas of proportion, the rise of mechanics, structural analysis, civil and military engineering, and new forms of drawing all change what architectural knowledge means.

This is a strong architectural case of [[Abstraction Without Prudence]]. The abstraction is not stupid or useless. Measurement, mechanics, geometry, and engineering are real forms of knowledge. The danger appears when they become the only legitimate knowledge architecture recognizes.

Once architecture is translated into technical representation, the older questions weaken: what world does the building disclose, what memory does it carry, what symbolic order does it inhabit, what kind of dwelling does it make possible?

## Geometry as Instrument

The book's deepest graph contribution is the story of geometry's demotion from symbolic generator of form to technical instrument. Geometry becomes increasingly useful for survey, calculation, projection, drawing, construction, and engineering, but less able to mediate a meaningful world.

This helps explain why later architecture can imagine dwelling as a system to optimize. When space is first understood as measurable, projectable, and technically available, it becomes easier to treat house, street, district, and city as design problems to be solved from the outside.

That makes the book a genealogy of [[Machine for Living]] rather than another example of it. Perez-Gomez does not give the house-machine slogan. He explains part of the intellectual path by which such a slogan could become persuasive.

## From Dwelling to Technical Object

The crisis named in the title is not that architecture learned too much science. The crisis is that architecture began to lose the ability to justify its poetic and symbolic work in a culture where objective technical knowledge seemed to be the only serious form of truth.

This places the book beside Roger Scruton's *[[The Aesthetics of Architecture]]*. Scruton asks philosophically why architecture cannot be reduced to utility, private taste, or function. Perez-Gomez asks historically how architecture arrived at a condition in which such reductions could seem natural.

Both works support [[Architecture as Public Language]], but from different directions. Scruton defends the public intelligibility of architecture as aesthetic judgment. Perez-Gomez shows how the technical-scientific worldview weakened architecture's older power to speak through memory, metaphor, number, and symbolic order.

That makes the book a deep genealogy of [[Place Replaced by Designed Environment]]. Before home can become equipment and place can become environment, architecture has to be reimagined as an object of technical representation rather than as a symbolic art of dwelling.

## The Background to Modern Functionalism

The book is useful for reading *[[Toward an Architecture]]*, *[[The New World (Hannes Meyer)]]*, and *[[Style and Epoch]]*. Those works present modern architecture as truthful because it answers machinery, function, economy, production, or the material reality of the age.

Perez-Gomez helps reveal what has already been conceded in that move. If truth in architecture is identified with technical exactness, functional adequacy, or the scientific description of need, then inherited symbol, ornament, ritual, craft, and metaphysical proportion can be dismissed as subjective or obsolete.

That is the deeper danger. Modern architecture may still seek beauty, clarity, social reform, and honesty. But if the framework has already reduced the building to technical object, those goods are forced to justify themselves inside a language that cannot fully receive them.

## Limits and Cautions

The book should not be used as a simple denunciation of science, engineering, or modern construction. Buildings must stand, materials must be understood, and measurement is not a fall from grace.

Its sharper use is to criticize the monopoly of technical reason. The question is not whether architecture should use science, but whether science has become the only authority through which architecture can understand itself.

The book can also tempt an overly romantic reading of premodern unity. For this graph, its strength is not nostalgia. Its strength is exposing the historical conditions under which architecture became vulnerable to technical reduction.

## Why It Belongs Here

*Architecture and the Crisis of Modern Science* belongs because it gives the architecture branch a deeper genealogy of modern technical domination. It shows that the city-machine, house-machine, social condenser, and functional plan are not only planning mistakes. They are late symptoms of an older crisis in which architecture loses confidence in its poetic, symbolic, and public meaning, then tries to survive by becoming scientific technique.
