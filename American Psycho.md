# American Psycho

***American Psycho*** is Bret Easton Ellis's 1991 novel about Patrick Bateman, a wealthy Wall Street professional whose life is composed of brands, restaurants, fitness, pornography, pop music, status anxiety, social sameness, and extreme violence.

## Personhood as Consumable Surface

Bateman's world is obsessively described through clothing labels, business cards, grooming, restaurants, electronics, music, and status objects. People often fail to recognize one another, and identity dissolves into interchangeable surfaces.

This connects the novel to [[Mediated Reality]]. Bateman does not simply live among commodities; he perceives and narrates reality through them. The image-world has become the practical environment of personhood.

## The Brand-Self and the Empty Interior

The novel's horror depends on the gap between polished surface and inward vacancy. Bateman appears as a successful social image, but the image does not reveal a stable person beneath it.

This places the book near [[Aesthetic Self-Enclosure]], though in a late commodity form rather than a decadent aristocratic one. The self is enclosed by surfaces, routines, consumption, and stylized performance.

The connection to [[Double and Divided Self]] is also strong. Bateman's public identity and violent interior do not integrate into a coherent soul. The division becomes so unstable that confession, recognition, and consequence all begin to fail.

## Violence as Consumer Event

The violence in *American Psycho* is not presented as passionate tragedy. It often appears flattened, repetitive, pornographic, catalogued, and tonally adjacent to consumer review.

This makes the novel a dark case of [[Aestheticized Transgression]]. The forbidden is not elevated into aristocratic style or technological ritual, but degraded into another form of consumption. The work's point is not that violence gives depth, but that a surface-world can turn even horror into consumable texture.

## The City of Surfaces

New York in the novel is a field of status signs, restaurants, offices, clubs, apartments, media, and fear. It is not only a setting for Bateman's crimes; it is the world that makes his image-legibility possible.

This connects the novel to [[Alienating City]]. The city functions as a pressure field where people become readable through money, brands, bodies, and social codes while becoming less recognizable as persons.

## Why It Belongs Here

*American Psycho* belongs because it gives the third face of this branch. *Crash* shows desire reprogrammed by automobile technology and injury. *Videodrome* shows perception and flesh reprogrammed by media signal. *American Psycho* shows personhood emptied by commodity surfaces until violence, status, pornography, and self-image become difficult to separate.
