# The Book of Urizen

***The Book of Urizen*** is William Blake's 1794 illuminated myth of Urizen, the lawmaking, measuring, restrictive figure who separates, codifies, and binds reality. It belongs in this graph because it gives a mythic form to reason turned prison: not modern bureaucracy, but the spiritual imagination of law, abstraction, and measurement as a false world-maker.

## Urizen as Restrictive Reason

Urizen embodies reason when it hardens into rule, boundary, system, and self-enclosed authority. He writes, measures, divides, and codifies, turning living reality into a closed order of law.

This places the work near [[Weaponized Rationalism]], but in a mythic key. Urizen is not a scientist or technocrat. He is a symbolic figure for reason severed from imagination, desire, body, and living relation.

## The False Creator Problem

Blake's myth can look Gnostic because Urizen resembles a false or diminished creator whose order traps life inside limitation. The world becomes a prison of law and division rather than a gift received in love.

That makes [[Gnosticism and its Critics]] a necessary bridge, with caution. Blake is not simply repeating ancient Gnosticism, but the work shares the pattern of a world-order associated with false creation, confinement, and the need for visionary liberation.

## Law Without Life

Urizen's order is not chaotic. Its horror is that it is orderly in a deadening way. The system remains coherent while losing the energy, freedom, and communion that would make order good.

This connects the poem to [[Hollowed Order]]. Blake's law is not merely strict; it is form emptied of living good, a structure that preserves control by reducing reality to what can be bounded.

## Repression and Revolutionary Energy

Urizen's binding of life helps explain why Blake associates sexual, imaginative, and revolutionary energies. Repression is not only a moral rule about bodies; it is part of a larger cosmic condition in which living energies are chained by a lawmaking power.

This gives the poem an indirect relation to [[Progress as Secular Providence]]. Blake's revolutionary hope is not rational perfectibility like Godwin's; it is the release of imaginative life from a deadened order.

## Why It Belongs Here

*The Book of Urizen* belongs because it supplies a mythic ancestor to later patterns of administered life. The graph usually sees dead reason through institutions, machines, or social systems. Blake imagines it as a fallen spiritual power that creates a world of law without life.
