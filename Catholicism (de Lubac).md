# Catholicism (de Lubac)

***Catholicism***, originally published as ***Catholicisme*** in 1938 and later translated as ***Catholicism: Christ and the Common Destiny of Man***, is Henri de Lubac's work on the social and communal dimensions of Catholic dogma. It belongs in this graph because it answers a recurring false choice: either the person is private and spiritual, or society is collective and political. De Lubac argues that Catholic salvation is personal and communal at once.

## Dogma Has Social Dimensions

De Lubac's key move is to show that Catholic doctrine is not a set of private propositions for isolated souls. Creation, sin, Israel, Christ, the Church, sacraments, and final destiny all have a social dimension.

This makes the work central to [[Ecclesial Communion]]. Christianity does not save individuals by extracting them from relation; it gathers them into a body.

## Against Individualist Religion

The book is useful because it resists a modern shrinking of religion into private inwardness. If salvation is ecclesial, sacramental, and cosmic, then Christianity cannot be reduced to personal consolation, moral taste, or inward sincerity.

This connects the work to [[Personalist Common Good]]. The person is not dissolved into a collective, but neither is he a sealed individual. He is made for communion.

## Against Secular Collectivism

De Lubac's communal theology is also not political collectivism. The Church is not a class, state, race, party, or administrative system. Communion has Christ as its center and grace as its life.

This helps distinguish Catholic sociality from [[Atheistic Communism]] and [[Religion of Humanity]]. Both can speak in collective terms, but de Lubac's communion is supernatural and ecclesial rather than immanent and political.

## Why It Belongs Here

*Catholicism* belongs because the graph contains many false unities: mass society, world state, collectivism, engineered consent, and systems that promise communion by administration. De Lubac gives a Catholic counter-form: true unity is not produced by power, but received in Christ through Church, sacrament, charity, and shared destiny.
