# Art and Scholasticism

***Art and Scholasticism*** is Jacques Maritain's 1920 work on art, beauty, making, prudence, and Thomistic aesthetics. It belongs in this graph because it gives a Catholic account of art as a real human making ordered by beauty without reducing art to propaganda, moralism, private sensation, or machine production.

## Art as Virtue of Making

Maritain treats art as a habit or virtue of the practical intellect concerned with making. Art has its own integrity as making; it is not simply a sermon, policy tool, or moral lesson in disguise.

This gives the book a strong relation to [[Reason Ordered to Truth]]. Making has intelligibility and order. Art is not irrational expression, but neither is it reducible to technical calculation.

## Beauty and Integrity

The book's Thomistic aesthetics helps the graph distinguish beauty from aesthetic self-enclosure. Beauty is not merely private intensity or decadent sensation. It belongs to being, form, radiance, and intelligible order.

This places the book near [[Beauty Returned to Worship]], though as art theory rather than conversion narrative. Maritain does not say every artwork must be explicitly religious; he gives a metaphysical account in which beauty remains answerable to reality.

## Art, Prudence, and Moral Order

Maritain distinguishes art from prudence without divorcing them absolutely. Art has its own good as making, but the artist remains a moral person.

This is useful for the graph's art branch. It avoids two errors at once: turning art into mere moral instruction, and treating artistic autonomy as exemption from truth, conscience, or the good.

That makes the book a neighbor of [[Leisure and Contemplation]] and [[Enchantment vs Magic]]. Good making receives and discloses reality rather than using form as manipulation.

## Why It Belongs Here

*Art and Scholasticism* belongs because the graph has many accounts of art becoming ideology, propaganda, transgression, design discipline, or self-enclosed aesthetic atmosphere. Maritain gives a Catholic theory of art that preserves artistic freedom while refusing to sever beauty from being, truth, and moral personhood.
