# Artificial Paradises

***Artificial Paradises*** (*Les Paradis artificiels*) is Baudelaire's 1860 work on hashish and opium, partly engaging Thomas De Quincey's *Confessions of an English Opium-Eater*. Its graph value is not drug history in itself, but the idea of counterfeit transcendence: a promised elevation that bypasses moral and spiritual transformation.

## Intoxication as False Ascent

Baudelaire treats intoxication as a temptation toward artificial elevation. It offers expansion, intensity, dream, and apparent escape from ordinary limits, but the ascent is unstable because it is not earned through conversion, discipline, grace, or relation.

This makes the work a strong non-technological case of [[False Cure]]. The wound is real: boredom, suffering, spiritual confinement, and the hunger for infinity. The cure is false because it gives an image of transcendence while leaving the self dependent and diminished.

## Technique Without Transformation

The work belongs near [[Aesthetic Self-Enclosure]] because intoxication becomes a technique for producing inward states. The self does not receive reality; it manufactures an experience.

That also gives the book a quiet relation to [[Domination Through the Machine]] by analogy, not by direct machinery. It shows a pre-technological version of a later pattern: the attempt to obtain transcendence through an apparatus, procedure, substance, or controlled stimulus.

## Desire for the Infinite

The most important link is [[Disordered Love]]. The desire for paradise is not mocked as stupid. It is a real desire for fullness. The disorder enters when the soul tries to possess paradise as sensation while avoiding the demands of truth.

This keeps the note connected to *[[Les Fleurs du mal]]* and *[[Paris Spleen]]*. Baudelaire's artificial paradise is another answer to spleen, but it cannot become a true answer.

## Why It Belongs Here

*Artificial Paradises* belongs because it helps the graph see false salvation before the age of technological systems. Later works offer soma, simulated worlds, engineered happiness, or machine immortality. Baudelaire gives the older inward form: paradise produced by chemical artifice and paid for by spiritual diminishment.
