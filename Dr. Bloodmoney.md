# Dr. Bloodmoney

***Dr. Bloodmoney, or How We Got Along After the Bomb*** is Philip K. Dick's 1965 post-nuclear novel about damaged communities, mutations, psychic powers, guilt, survival, and strange forms of ordinary life after catastrophe. It is not a clean technocratic dystopia. Its value for the graph is that it makes nuclear aftermath weird, social, bodily, and morally unstable rather than merely spectacular.

## The Bomb as Moral and Bodily Disorder

The catastrophe in *Dr. Bloodmoney* is not only a change of setting. The world after the bomb is full of wounded bodies, altered capacities, social improvisation, and half-comic attempts to live with irreversible damage.

This connects the novel to [[World Wound and Long Decline]]. The wound is not just rubble; it enters bodies, families, towns, memory, and ordinary trust.

## Scientific Power Without Providential Wisdom

The figure of Dr. Bluthgeld gives the novel its strongest link to this branch. His scientific role is joined to guilt, catastrophe, paranoia, and an inability to place his power inside a moral order.

This touches [[Sterile Scientific Salvation]] only obliquely. The novel is not centered on a laboratory program that redesigns humanity after the war. Its point is stranger: scientific power has helped wound the world, but the postwar world must be inhabited by damaged persons rather than solved by the same kind of power that helped produce the damage.

## Mutation Without a Program

Unlike *[[Fallout]]*, the altered beings and unusual powers in *Dr. Bloodmoney* do not form a neat scheme of forced evolution. Mutation is not presented as a clean route to successor humanity.

That distinction matters. The novel is useful as a negative comparison to the successor-humanity motif: it shows post-nuclear alteration without turning that alteration into a confident program of purification, completion, or replacement.

## Communities After Expert Failure

The novel's local communities matter because they are not grand systems. People barter, adapt, fear one another, tell stories, and keep partial social forms alive after the official world has failed.

This gives the book a fragile counterweight to false salvation by system. Survival is awkward, comic, damaged, and local. It is not the achievement of a perfected scientific order.

## Why It Belongs Here

*Dr. Bloodmoney* belongs because it deepens the nuclear branch behind *[[Fallout]]* and *[[Wasteland]]*. It shows a postwar world where science has not saved humanity, but has left humanity to live among bodily wounds, strange gifts, guilt, and broken forms of community.
