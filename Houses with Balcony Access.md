# Houses with Balcony Access

The Houses with Balcony Access in Dessau-Törten are five apartment blocks built in 1929-1930 under Hannes Meyer and the Bauhaus architecture department as social housing near Walter Gropius's earlier Törten Housing Estate. They belong in the graph because Meyer's social-functional program enters ordinary domestic life: minimum dwelling, sunlight, economy, shared access, gardens, laundry, and communal encounter are arranged as a rational housing system.

This is the Bauhaus not as manifesto, school icon, or labor-training campus, but as workers' housing.

## Source Base

This note reads the Houses with Balcony Access as built social housing through the Bauhaus Dessau Foundation, Bauhaus Kooperation, UNESCO's Bauhaus World Heritage framing, and Meyer's theoretical context. The buildings do not speak like a book, so the interpretation should remain tied to plan, commission, use, housing standard, access system, construction economy, and later preservation.

They are especially important because they were planned and implemented by the Bauhaus architecture department under Meyer, unlike Gropius's earlier Törten housing estate, which came through his private office. The difference makes these houses one of the clearer collective Bauhaus building projects.

## People's Needs Instead of Luxury Needs

Meyer's slogan, often rendered as "people's needs rather than luxury needs," gives the project its moral pressure. The houses were not luxury modernism. They were meant to provide economical, functional dwellings for lower-income workers and employees.

This makes the project a built continuation of *[[The New World (Hannes Meyer)]].* In the essay, modern housing is a technical and social process shaped by light, heating, hygiene, family life, economy, materials, and collective need. In Dessau-Törten, that becomes a modest apartment type.

The promise should be taken seriously. The project tries to answer real housing scarcity and class inequality through careful use of plan, cost, sunlight, sanitation, and shared amenities.

## Minimum Dwelling as Domestic System

The apartments were small and economical, but they included modern domestic equipment: rooms organized for family use, kitchen, bathroom, toilet, heating, sunlight, and service spaces. The point was not expressive individuality, but sufficient, rational, healthy dwelling.

This makes the houses a strong case of [[Machine for Living]] in the social-housing register. Dwelling is understood as a system of needs to be met by plan, material, orientation, cost, and shared infrastructure.

The danger is the same one that runs through the architecture branch. The minimum dwelling can protect the poor from unhealthy conditions, but it can also define the household from above as a calculable unit of minimum requirements.

## The Balcony Corridor as Street Substitute

The access balcony is the project's most revealing element. It saves money by reducing internal circulation, but it also creates a semi-public zone where neighbors can meet, linger, and be seen.

This gives the houses a more complicated relation to [[Embodied Relation]] than many high-modernist plans. The balcony corridor is not simply an abstract diagram. It tries to reintroduce public encounter into economical housing, almost as a raised substitute for the pavement or street.

The question is whether such a substitute can carry the full thickness of ordinary street life. It can encourage contact, but it is still designed as a controlled access system rather than an inherited public realm.

## Sun, Garden, Laundry, Play

The houses were arranged so that living spaces could face the sun, with access and secondary rooms to the north. Courtyards, children's play areas, kitchen gardens, washhouses, and laundry spaces made the housing more than a row of private boxes.

This connects the project to [[Administered Human Formation]] in a domestic and benevolent form. The environment shapes household routine: washing, gardening, childcare, sun exposure, neighborly encounter, and movement through shared spaces.

The point is not coercion. The point is that social reform becomes architectural arrangement. Better life is sought through the designed order of domestic repetition.

## Collective Bauhaus Housing

The project also matters institutionally. It was a joint Bauhaus architecture-department project, and its planning involved Meyer, students, collaborators, and an urban concept associated with Ludwig Hilberseimer.

This connects the houses back to [[Bauhaus Manifesto and Program]], but in a transformed way. The founding dream of arts united around building becomes low-cost housing, local materials, standardized elements, and collective design labor.

The cathedral image has disappeared. What remains is the social task of housing.

## Why It Belongs Here

The Houses with Balcony Access belong in the graph because they show modernist social reform entering the ordinary dwelling. They are humane, economical, and serious, but they also reveal how deeply modern architecture can reorganize daily life: household, neighbor, street, sun, laundry, garden, cost, and family are all translated into design problems. They are one of the clearest domestic cases of the Bauhaus social-functional program.
