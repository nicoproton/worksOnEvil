# Personalist Common Good

Personalist Common Good names the Catholic pattern in which the person is neither an isolated individual nor a mere part of the collective. The common good is real and binding, but it exists for persons who have spiritual dignity, freedom, truth, and a final end beyond the state, market, class, or party.

This bridge belongs near [[Christian Civil Order]], [[Subsidiarity]], and [[Economic Order Under Moral Law]]. It is useful when the graph needs to distinguish Catholic social thought from both liberal individualism and collectivist absorption.

Use this link when:

- the person is defended against being reduced to atom, consumer, worker, patient, citizen-function, class-unit, or state material;
- the common good is affirmed without making the collectivity absolute;
- society is ordered toward personal flourishing rather than private self-will or total administration;
- rights, duties, institutions, and authority are grounded in human dignity and moral order;
- the text distinguishes true community from both individualism and totalitarian unity.

Jacques Maritain's *[[The Person and the Common Good]]* is the central theoretical anchor. Maritain distinguishes individuality from personality so that man can belong to the political community without being exhausted by it.

Maritain's *[[Integral Humanism]]* gives the broader political-cultural form. It seeks a Christian humanism adequate to plural modern society without reducing man to secular immanence.

Romano Guardini's *[[The World and the Person]]* gives a neighboring anthropological form. The person is not an isolated consciousness, but neither is he a function of world, state, culture, or system.

Henri de Lubac's *[[Catholicism (de Lubac)]]* gives the ecclesial-theological neighbor. The person is made for communion, but that communion is not state, class, race, or Humanity worship; it is ordered through Christ and the Church.

Yves Congar's [[Lay Vocation]] gives the worldly-ecclesial neighbor. The person serves the common good not only by belonging to institutions, but by responsible Christian action in ordinary family, work, culture, and civic life.

This bridge is a counter-form to [[Atheistic Communism]], where the person is absorbed into materialist class and collective history. It is also a counter-form to [[Affluent Nihilism]], where the person is reduced more softly to consumer choice, comfort, and private self-definition.

This concept should not be used for every appeal to dignity or community. It belongs where the text holds together both poles: the person is social and ordered to the common good, yet cannot be possessed by society as a mere part.
