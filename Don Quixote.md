# Don Quixote

***Don Quixote*** is Miguel de Cervantes's 1605/1615 novel about Alonso Quixano, a country gentleman whose obsessive reading of chivalric romances leads him to become the knight-errant Don Quixote. It belongs in this graph as the great ancestor of the borrowed-life pattern: books do not merely entertain him, but teach him how to misrecognize the world.

## Chivalric Romance as World-Filter

Don Quixote does not only imitate stories. He sees through them. Inns become castles, windmills become giants, ordinary women become ladies of romance, and practical situations become occasions for knightly adventure.

This makes the novel a foundational case for [[Bovarysme]]. The self is reorganized around a borrowed script, and reality is judged according to whether it can bear the role assigned to it.

## A Noble Error

The novel is not a simple attack on imagination. Don Quixote's delusion is comic and destructive, but it is also bound to real longings: justice, honor, courage, fidelity, and the desire for life to be more than appetite and calculation.

That ambiguity matters for the graph. Compared with *[[Madame Bovary]]*, Don Quixote's borrowed world is less erotic and consumerist. His fantasy is chivalric and moralized. The problem is not that he desires justice, but that his desire has lost truthful contact with actual persons and situations.

## Sancho and the Resistance of Reality

Sancho Panza keeps the book from becoming pure fantasy. His bodily needs, sayings, prudence, greed, loyalty, and comic literalness repeatedly pull the story back toward earth.

This contrast gives the novel its depth. The borrowed ideal and the stubborn real world are not simply enemies. They correct, wound, and expose one another.

## Why It Belongs Here

*Don Quixote* belongs because it shows that mediated reality begins long before electronic media. A library can become an operating environment for the soul. The later bourgeois and decadent versions of the pattern are narrower; Cervantes gives the archetypal comic form.
