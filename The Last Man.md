# The Last Man

***The Last Man*** is Mary Shelley's 1826 apocalyptic novel about a future world devastated by plague until human civilization collapses and Lionel Verney is left as the apparent last survivor. It belongs in this graph as a dark counterweight to Romantic political hope: systems, friendships, nations, and heroic ideals fail before mortality and historical loss.

## Plague Against Political Hope

The novel begins in a future shaped by political aspiration, reform, friendship, and public life. But the plague breaks the confidence that history can be secured by institutions or heroic will.

This gives the book a strong relation to [[False Salvation Through Systems]] by negation. No system saves the world here. The novel exposes how fragile political and civilizational projects become when confronted with death on a planetary scale.

## The End of the Social World

As the plague advances, society does not only lose population. It loses continuity, memory, ritual, shared labor, and the ordinary forms that make human beings intelligible to one another.

That places the novel near [[World Wound and Long Decline]]. The wound is historical and communal: the world becomes less able to hold human meaning because the bonds that transmit meaning are dying.

## Romantic Solitude

Lionel Verney's survival is not triumph. To be the last man is to possess memory without community. The self remains, but the world that could answer it has disappeared.

This makes the novel useful beside *[[Frankenstein]]*. Mary Shelley returns to isolation, but on a civilizational scale. Victor's Arctic solitude becomes, in *The Last Man*, the solitude of humanity after its own hopes have vanished.

## Why It Belongs Here

*The Last Man* belongs because it keeps the Shelley branch from becoming only Promethean. It shows the collapse of political and Romantic confidence before death, contingency, and the fragility of embodied community.
