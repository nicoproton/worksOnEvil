# Reflections on Violence

***Reflections on Violence*** is Georges Sorel's 1908 book on revolutionary syndicalism, violence, class struggle, and the mobilizing force of myth. It belongs in this graph because it gives the political-mythic side of modern reconstruction: people are moved not only by rational programs, but by images of decisive action and historical rupture.

Sorel is not an architect or artist, but his importance for the graph is cross-domain. His theory of myth helps explain why modern politics, art, theater, propaganda, and architecture can become charged with images of renewal through breakage.

## Myth as Mobilizing Form

Sorel's central contribution is the idea that political myth is not merely falsehood. It is an image capable of organizing will, action, discipline, and collective identity.

This places the work under [[Life Redesigned by Ideology]]. The social order is not transformed only by policy. It is transformed by the formation of desire, imagination, and militant expectation.

## Violence and Rupture

The work treats violence as a force that can separate revolutionary action from parliamentary compromise and bourgeois comfort.

This makes it a political neighbor to *[[The Futurist Manifesto]]*. Futurism aestheticizes speed, danger, youth, war, and destruction. Sorel gives a political theory in which rupture and action can become morally purifying.

The connection should remain careful. Futurism is not simply Sorelian politics, and Sorel is not simply Futurist art. The shared point is the modern attraction to violent discontinuity as a path to renewal.

## Across Left and Right

Sorel matters because his ideas could be received across ideological camps. Revolutionary syndicalists, anti-liberal radicals, and later movements on both left and right could draw on myth, action, production, and anti-parliamentary energy.

This helps explain why similar modernist forms can travel between enemy ideologies. The shared enemy may be inherited liberal, bourgeois, parliamentary, or traditional order, even when the proposed replacement differs sharply.

## Why It Belongs Here

*Reflections on Violence* belongs because it gives the graph a non-architectural source for modernism's rhetoric of rupture. It helps connect politics, art, theater, propaganda, and planning through the belief that the old world must be broken before the new one can appear.
