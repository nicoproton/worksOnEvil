# Bovarysme

Bovarysme names the pattern in which a person lives through an imagined self shaped by borrowed images, romantic scripts, social fantasies, books, fashions, or aesthetic poses. The person does not merely desire something false. The deeper problem is that desire has learned to want life as an image of life.

This bridge belongs near [[Mediated Reality]], [[Disordered Love]], and [[Aesthetic Self-Enclosure]]. It is useful when a character's relation to reality is filtered through novels, consumer objects, theatrical self-image, decadent taste, or fantasies of a more intense life.

Use this link when:

- a character measures ordinary life against borrowed romantic or aesthetic scripts;
- reading, style, fashion, objects, or social fantasy create a false image of the self;
- dissatisfaction with ordinary limits becomes a refusal to receive actual persons and duties;
- beauty, passion, or refinement becomes a substitute for reality rather than a way of entering reality more deeply.

*[[Madame Bovary]]* is the central case. Emma's imagination has been formed by romantic literature, aristocratic images, Catholic-romantic sentiment, luxury goods, and fantasies of passionate escape. She does not merely commit adultery or spend beyond her means; she tries to inhabit a life that has already been scripted elsewhere.

*[[Don Quixote]]* gives the comic and chivalric ancestor: reading reorganizes reality until ordinary events are forced into a heroic script. *[[The Sorrows of Young Werther]]* gives the sentimental form, where feeling and impossible love become the measure of life. *[[Sentimental Education]]* gives Flaubert's male and historical form, where romantic self-imagination becomes drift rather than open catastrophe.

*[[Lost Illusions]]* gives the public and media form: the imagined self enters a machinery of journalism, reputation, and social ascent. *[[La Regenta]]* gives the Spanish provincial form, where romantic fantasy is joined to religious hunger, corrupt guidance, and social surveillance.

*[[Venus in Furs]]* gives the contractual and theatrical form: desire is shaped by art, myth, costume, and a role that the protagonist tries to make real.

*[[Against Nature (A Rebours)]]* gives a later decadent and aristocratic form. Des Esseintes knows he is constructing an artificial world, while Emma experiences her borrowed fantasies as promises that life itself has failed to keep. *[[The Picture of Dorian Gray]]* gives the Gothic-aesthetic form: Dorian receives a book and a philosophy that let him imagine life as curated sensation while consequence is hidden elsewhere.

This concept is related to [[Simulated Reality]], but it is more ordinary and literary. No machine needs to manufacture the false world. Books, phrases, interiors, commodities, and social images can become enough.
