# Die Macht

***Die Macht*** is Romano Guardini's 1951 postwar reflection on power, responsibility, technical capacity, and moral formation. It belongs in this graph because it sharpens one of Guardini's recurring claims: modern man has gained new powers without an equal growth in the wisdom needed to govern them.

## Power Has Become Structural

Guardini is not speaking only about rulers or weapons. Modern power is distributed through technology, administration, industry, media, planning, science, and institutional systems. It becomes part of the environment in which ordinary life happens.

This connects the work to [[Total Technical Environment]]. Power is no longer merely an act of command from outside; it becomes the shape of conditions, habits, dependencies, and available possibilities.

## Responsibility Must Grow With Power

The book's Catholic importance is the demand for moral and spiritual responsibility. Power is not justified by efficiency, novelty, or historical inevitability. It must be answered for before truth, conscience, neighbor, creation, and God.

This places the work near [[Domination Through the Machine]]. The machine-logic of domination expands the will's reach; Guardini asks whether the person has the interior order needed not to become servant of his own instruments.

## Power Without Reverence

Guardini's warning also touches [[Weaponized Rationalism]]. Reason and technique become dangerous when they narrow reality into what can be calculated, manipulated, and put to use. The problem is not reason itself, but reason severed from reverence and responsibility.

The work should not be used as a simple anti-technology node. Guardini's point is more demanding: new power requires a deeper person, not merely less machinery.

## Why It Belongs Here

*Die Macht* is worth adding because it makes explicit what is implicit in *[[The End of the Modern World]]*: the crisis of modernity is a crisis of power without corresponding moral formation. It gives the graph a Catholic non-fiction bridge between technological dystopia, institutional control, and the spiritual question of what power is for.
