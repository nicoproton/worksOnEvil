# Machine-Age Standardization

Machine-Age Standardization names the modernist pattern in which industrial production, standardized types, engineering clarity, mass manufacture, economy, and elimination of waste become architectural and social virtues.

This bridge belongs near [[Machine for Living]], [[Life Redesigned by Ideology]], and [[Domination Through the Machine]]. It is useful when the machine is not only a tool but a moral image: disciplined, efficient, exact, reproducible, anti-ornamental, and supposedly democratic.

## Use This Link When

- ships, airplanes, automobiles, factories, tools, or standard objects become models for architecture;
- mass production and standard types are treated as liberating or socially progressive;
- ornament, craft variation, or inherited style is criticized as waste, costume, or confusion;
- architecture begins to judge dwelling by efficiency, hygiene, reproducibility, and technical clarity;
- the danger is not machinery itself, but the reduction of dwelling and public form to industrial method.

## Sources and Forms

*[[Toward an Architecture]]* is the central architectural manifesto for this bridge. Its praise of engineers, standard objects, ships, airplanes, automobiles, and the house-machine makes standardization appear beautiful, rational, and socially urgent.

*[[Apres le Cubisme]]* and *[[L'Esprit Nouveau]]* give the Purist art-theory background: standard objects, clear form, order, and modern visual discipline enter architecture before they become planning doctrine.

Taylor's *[[The Principles of Scientific Management]]* and Ford's *[[Today and Tomorrow]]* give the industrial atmosphere: labor, production, time, waste, and flow become objects of analysis and redesign.

*[[Theory and Design in the First Machine Age]]* is a useful critical genealogy because Banham distinguishes actual machine production from the symbolic prestige of the machine in modernist theory.

## Why It Belongs Here

Machine-Age Standardization helps reduce graph noise in the architecture branch. It lets works meet through a shared industrial logic instead of linking every modernist manifesto directly to every other manifesto, factory text, Purist text, or later planning document.
