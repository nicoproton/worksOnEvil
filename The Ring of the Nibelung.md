# The Ring of the Nibelung

***The Ring of the Nibelung*** (*Der Ring des Nibelungen*) is Richard Wagner's four-opera mythic cycle about the theft of Rhine gold, the forging of a world-ruling ring, the curse attached to it, and the downfall of gods, heroes, and old order. It belongs in this graph because it joins power, renunciation of love, forged domination, inherited curse, and world-ending renewal.

## The Ring Made by Renouncing Love

The ring's origin is already a moral diagnosis. Alberich can forge it only by renouncing love. Power over the world begins with a wound in desire.

This makes the cycle a strong case for [[Disordered Love]]. The lust for possession does not merely add power to life; it begins by cutting the maker off from the good that would have ordered desire.

## Forged Power and Domination

The ring is an artifact of command. It concentrates desire into a tool that promises mastery over others and binds its possessors into fear, rivalry, and violence.

This places the cycle near [[Domination Through the Machine]] in a mythic rather than industrial sense. The ring is not a modern technology, but it is a device that makes possessive will effective.

## Contracts, Gods, and Hollow Order

Wotan's world depends on law, contracts, bargains, and authority, but these forms cannot heal the disorder beneath them. The divine order itself becomes entangled in debt and compromise.

That gives the cycle a relation to [[Hollowed Order]]. The forms of rule and obligation remain, yet they no longer secure justice or peace because the order has been founded on possession and evasion.

## Curse and World Decline

The curse spreads through gods, giants, dwarfs, heroes, and families. The world does not merely contain a dangerous object; the object exposes a whole order already vulnerable to greed, fear, and broken trust.

This connects the cycle to [[World Wound and Long Decline]]. Wagner's mythic world moves toward catastrophe because power has entered the roots of relation.

## Tolkien and the Shared Northern Inheritance

The comparison with *[[The Lord of the Rings]]* is necessary but should be handled carefully. Tolkien was a philologist deeply engaged with Northern legend, and Wagner also drew on Germanic and Norse materials. Tolkien explicitly rejected a simple equation between the two rings, writing that both were round and that the resemblance ended there.

The better graph relation is contrast through shared mythic material. Wagner's ring belongs to a world of tragic power, erotic renunciation, and the collapse of divine order. Tolkien's Ring is Sauron's externalized will, a non-modern "Machine" whose only cure is renunciation, pity, and providence rather than heroic possession.

## Why It Belongs Here

*The Ring of the Nibelung* belongs because it gives the graph a monumental mythic case of power bought by the renunciation of love. It also clarifies Tolkien by contrast: two ring-myths can share sources while making radically different theological and moral claims.
