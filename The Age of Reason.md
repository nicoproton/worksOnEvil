# The Age of Reason

***The Age of Reason*** is Thomas Paine's 1794-1795 deist critique of revealed religion, biblical authority, miracles, priestcraft, and institutional Christianity. It belongs in this graph because it gives the public rationalist attack on revelation: God may remain, but supernatural tradition is judged before the tribunal of reason.

## Deism Against Revelation

Paine does not argue as a materialist atheist. He affirms God through nature and reason while rejecting revelation, Scripture's special authority, and ecclesiastical mediation.

This places the work near [[Progress as Secular Providence]]. Religion is not replaced by machinery or occult knowledge, but by a rational natural religion that treats historical revelation as error, imposture, or superstition.

## Priestcraft and Hollowed Religion

For Paine, priestly authority and revealed systems turn religion into domination. Christianity's institutional claims appear as forms whose divine warrant has been hollowed out by contradiction, cruelty, and superstition.

This connects the work to [[Hollowed Order]]. The link should be read from Paine's argument, not as the graph's final judgment: Paine says inherited religious form no longer carries truth, while Christian writers in the vault often argue that modern substitutes hollow the form even further.

## Reason as Judge

The work is an important contrast with *[[The Abolition of Man]]*. Lewis criticizes the reduction of inherited moral and spiritual order to subjective or historical construction; Paine subjects inherited revelation to public rational scrutiny.

That makes the book relevant to [[Weaponized Rationalism]] only by contrast. Paine's reason is polemical and anti-clerical, not a technique of bureaucratic domination, but it participates in the larger modern movement that places inherited order under rational audit.

## Why It Belongs Here

*The Age of Reason* belongs because it gives the graph a clear deist anti-revelation node. It helps distinguish Enlightenment rational religion from later occultism, positivist religion, and technocratic social planning.
