# Backrooms

***Backrooms*** is Kane Parsons's 2026 horror film for A24, growing out of the earlier Backrooms internet myth and Parsons's YouTube found-footage series. It belongs in this graph because it makes architecture itself the horror: not a haunted house with memory, not a city with social pressure, but an endless designed environment whose ordinary forms have lost every human purpose.

The work is layered: a creepypasta image-and-text myth, a participatory online mythology, a YouTube analog-horror expansion, and then a feature film. Its strongest graph connection is architectural. The Backrooms are frightening because they look like familiar modern interiors after placehood has drained away.

## Place Without Place

The Backrooms are made from ordinary non-places: office corridors, showroom basements, drop ceilings, wallpaper, carpet, fluorescent light, utility spaces, and endless rooms that feel designed but not inhabited.

This makes the work one of the clearest horror cases of [[Place Replaced by Designed Environment]]. The space is not wild, Gothic, ancient, sacred, or sublime. It is modern interior space after function has collapsed. It still has the signs of use, maintenance, circulation, and commercial or institutional order, but no meaningful use remains.

The result is not simply ugliness. It is a place that cannot become a place. There is no street, threshold, hearth, neighbor, civic front, shrine, window-view, local memory, or public language. There is only environment.

## Architecture After Public Language

The Backrooms are a nightmare image of what [[Architecture as Public Language]] fears. Architecture usually tells human beings where they are: entrance, exit, room, corridor, facade, private, public, sacred, domestic, commercial, civic.

In *Backrooms*, those signs remain only as fragments. A room looks like a room, but it does not gather anyone. A corridor suggests movement, but not destination. Fluorescent light suggests work or safety, but not care. Wallpaper suggests interior comfort, but becomes hostile through repetition.

The horror therefore comes from a kind of architectural aphasia. The space speaks in the grammar of offices and commercial interiors, but the sentence has no meaning.

## Hollowed Modern Order

This also connects the work to [[Hollowed Order]]. The Backrooms preserve recognizable modern forms after their animating goods have vanished. The office remains without work. The showroom remains without commerce. The corridor remains without arrival. The safe interior remains without shelter.

The pattern is close to privation, but the note should be careful. *Backrooms* does not present an explicit theology of evil. Its horror is experiential: familiar order persists after the human goods that made it intelligible have been removed.

This is why the space feels more disturbing than a ruin. A ruin at least belongs to time. The Backrooms feel like a designed present that cannot age, remember, or end.

## No-Clipping and the Broken Boundary

The Backrooms myth uses the video-game language of "no-clipping": slipping through the normal boundaries of reality as if the world has seams. This gives the work a light connection to [[Simulated Reality]], but it should not be treated as a simple simulation story.

The stronger point is architectural and ontological. The wall, floor, basement doorway, or threshold stops being a stable boundary between rooms and becomes an accidental wound in the world. Normal space is revealed as less secure than it looked.

That also keeps *Backrooms* distinct from [[Total Technical Environment]]. The space may feel industrial, artificial, and technically produced, but the horror is not a functioning machine system in the manner of *The Machine Stops* or *BLAME!*. It is the remains of designed environment after design no longer serves dwelling.

## Internet Myth and Found Footage

The Backrooms began as an image and a copied story before becoming Parsons's analog-horror series and the later film. That origin matters because the space is encountered through [[Mediated Reality]] before it is encountered as a fictional place.

The photograph, forum text, creepypasta expansion, YouTube found footage, VHS texture, and online lore all make the Backrooms feel discovered rather than authored. The myth works because it appears as documentation of a hidden environment that might already exist beneath familiar modern interiors.

This gives *Backrooms* a different relation to media than *Videodrome* or *Serial Experiments Lain*. The media are not the ultimate god or networked cosmos. They are the way an architectural nightmare becomes collectively believable.

## Not the Alienating City, But Its Interior Residue

*Backrooms* touches [[Alienating City]], but indirectly. It is not a city crowded with strangers, advertisements, vehicles, police, screens, and money. It is what remains when the modern institutional interior is isolated from the city and deprived of use.

That makes it a more distilled horror than many urban works. The city is gone, but its low-grade interiors remain: offices, commercial rooms, service corridors, waiting areas, storage spaces, and transitional zones. The social world has disappeared, leaving only the atmosphere of a world built for circulation, work, management, and consumption.

Reinhold Martin's *The Organizational Complex* gives a non-fiction neighbor for this atmosphere. It is not a source for *Backrooms*, but it helps explain why postwar corporate offices, modular interiors, service spaces, media images, and organizational environments can feel spiritually charged before horror turns them into an endless place without place.

## Lynchian Neighbor, But Not the Same Pattern

David Lynch's work is a useful neighbor through [[Threshold Room]]. *Twin Peaks*, *Mulholland Drive*, *Lost Highway*, and *Inland Empire* all use rooms, theaters, corridors, stages, or homes as passages into dream, guilt, image, or spiritual pressure.

The distinction matters. Lynch's rooms are usually overcharged with symbol, performance, desire, and hidden presence. The Backrooms are frightening because the designed interior has become too empty, too repeatable, and too deprived of ordinary human meaning.

## Why It Belongs Here

*Backrooms* belongs because it gives the graph a contemporary horror image for the architecture branch. It shows the designed environment after public language, memory, use, and dwelling have failed. Modern interior space becomes [[Architectural Uncanny]] not because it is strange, but because it is too familiar and too empty: a human-made environment from which the human meaning has been evacuated.
