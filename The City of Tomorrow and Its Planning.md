# The City of Tomorrow and Its Planning

***The City of Tomorrow and Its Planning*** (*Urbanisme*, 1925) is Le Corbusier's major urban-planning work, extending the machine-age architectural vision of *[[Toward an Architecture]]* from the house to the city. It argues for order, traffic rationalization, high-rise towers, open space, hygiene, zoning, and a new urban form adequate to modern life.

The book matters for this graph because it makes the transition from dwelling as technical object to city as technical system. The planner does not merely design buildings. He proposes a new spatial order in which circulation, residence, work, administration, density, and health are reorganized from above.

## From House-Machine to City-Machine

In *Toward an Architecture*, Le Corbusier gives the house-machine its manifesto form. In *The City of Tomorrow*, the same logic expands outward. The city itself becomes a coordinated apparatus: streets, towers, green space, traffic, density, and daily movement are treated as parts of one rational design.

This makes the book a major development of [[Machine for Living]]. The question is no longer only whether a house can be modern, hygienic, and efficient. The question is whether urban life as a whole can be rebuilt as a functional mechanism for modern persons.

The promise is health, clarity, light, open ground, efficient movement, and release from congested inherited streets. The danger is that the city begins to be understood more as a diagram of functions than as an accumulated human dwelling.

## The View From Above

The planning imagination of the book depends on seeing the city from a commanding distance. Old urban fabric appears tangled, congested, and irrational; the new plan appears clear, geometric, and legible.

This gives the book a strong relation to [[High-Modernist Planning]]. The city is not only improved locally. It is simplified and reorganized according to principles that can be grasped from above: axes, towers, arterial roads, separation of functions, and abstract order.

That top-down clarity is the source of both power and danger. It can reveal real problems hidden by habit, but it can also flatten the local knowledge, informal life, memory, and small-scale dependence through which a city actually lives.

## Traffic, Straight Lines, and Circulation

Traffic is one of the book's controlling problems. The old street is inadequate to the speed and quantity of modern movement. The new city must separate, direct, and accelerate circulation.

This places the work near [[Domination Through the Machine]]. The machine is not only the automobile. It is the whole technical logic by which city life is reconfigured around movement, flow, and efficiency.

The pedestrian city, the market street, and the irregular neighborhood become suspect because they obstruct circulation. In this sense, the city is judged by a standard that comes from traffic and technical order rather than from the slow relationships of ordinary dwelling.

## Towers, Open Space, and the Purified City

Le Corbusier's tower city promises density without congestion. By building upward and freeing the ground, the plan offers light, air, greenery, and order. This is not merely aesthetic minimalism; it is a social and hygienic claim.

This is why the book belongs near [[False Salvation Through Systems]] only with care. The promised goods are real: healthier housing, sunlight, air, and relief from slum conditions. But the salvific tone of the system is dangerous when it implies that human disorder can be solved by spatial purification.

The purification is architectural before it is moral: remove clutter, old fabric, irregular streets, unhealthy density, and confused functions; install towers, light, air, and rational circulation. The city is healed by being remade as a system.

## Plan Voisin as the Revealing Project

The book's most famous associated project is [[Plan Voisin]], Le Corbusier's proposal for central Paris. Its importance lies in making the abstract doctrine visible: large areas of inherited urban fabric are treated as replaceable by cruciform towers, broad arteries, and open space.

This connection should be kept direct because it is not merely thematic. *The City of Tomorrow* provides the conceptual language; Plan Voisin is the project-image that reveals the force of the language when applied to an existing city.

The later *[[The Radiant City]]* generalizes this urban imagination into a more portable doctrine of housing, green space, traffic separation, services, and planned modern life.

## Formation Through Environment

The city of tomorrow is not neutral infrastructure. It forms habits. A city organized by towers, zoning, traffic, parks, and functional separation trains persons to live in certain ways.

This connects the book to [[Administered Human Formation]]. Le Corbusier is not writing a school curriculum, but he imagines urban form as a force that can produce healthier, clearer, more modern life.

The question for the graph is whether this formation remains humane. Does the city form persons by giving them a better environment, or does it make persons legible as units inside a designed order?

## Against the Inherited City

The book's deepest tension is its relation to inheritance. The old city is not only physically crowded; it is an archive of memory, mixed use, class contact, accidents, local adaptations, and symbolic depth. Le Corbusier often sees these accumulations as obstacles to order.

This places the work in argument with [[Embodied Relation]] and with craft-centered notes such as *[[The Nature of Gothic]]*. Ruskin finds moral life in irregular, embodied, hand-made order. Le Corbusier seeks moral renewal through geometric clarity, standardization, and technical simplification.

Jane Jacobs's *[[The Death and Life of Great American Cities]]* gives the later urban answer. Her defense of street life, mixed use, small blocks, aged buildings, density, and sidewalk trust directly challenges the kind of top-down clarity that Le Corbusier makes attractive.

## Why It Belongs Here

*The City of Tomorrow and Its Planning* belongs in the graph because it shows architecture becoming urban social engineering. It is not only a book about buildings. It is a book about how the modern city might be made legible, efficient, hygienic, and administrable, and therefore how human life might be spatially re-formed by the planner's idea of order.
