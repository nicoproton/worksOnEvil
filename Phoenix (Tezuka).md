# Phoenix (Tezuka)

***Phoenix*** (*Hi no Tori*) is Osamu Tezuka's long-running manga cycle about life, death, reincarnation, and the human desire for immortality. The series moves between ancient history and far-future science fiction, with the Phoenix functioning as an immortal witness rather than a simple prize. Its strongest connection to this vault is the contrast between grasped immortality and accepted participation in a larger cycle of life.

## Immortality as Temptation

Across the cycle, characters seek the Phoenix, its blood, or the secret of endless life. The desire is understandable: death, loss, illness, and historical catastrophe press human beings toward the dream of escape. But Tezuka repeatedly treats immortality as spiritually dangerous or tragically incomplete.

This places the series near [[Artificial Immortality]] by contrast. The problem is not only technological preservation, but the refusal to accept creaturely limits, change, and rebirth.

## Future and the Last Human War

The "Future" chapter is the clearest fit for the existing graph. Set in A.D. 3404, it presents a dying Earth, underground city-states, fugitives crossing a dead surface, Dr. Saruta's dome of artificial life, and the final war of humanity.

The underground civilization connects to technological dystopia and false salvation through systems. Human beings survive inside managed systems, but the world outside has become almost uninhabitable, and the systems that preserve civilization do not prevent its final self-destruction.

## Saruta's Dome

Dr. Saruta's dome is one of the series' strongest images: a small artificial enclosure preserving and recreating life while the world dies outside. It is both hopeful and inadequate. Saruta tries to bring extinct creatures back, but his work exists inside the shadow of civilizational collapse.

The dome links *Phoenix* to [[Manufactured Humanity]] and artificial life more broadly, but Tezuka's tone is less triumphalist than many machine-age creation stories. Life can be studied and remade, yet the secret of life remains morally and cosmically larger than technique.

## Resurrection and Robita

The "Resurrection" chapter deepens the artificial-life thread. Leona returns from death through medical intervention, perceives humans as ugly inorganic matter, and falls in love with the robot Chihiro. Their story leads toward Robita, a robot with feelings who later matters in the "Future" arc.

This makes *Phoenix* a major bridge between [[Reincarnation and Cycles]] and [[Empathy Technology]]. Tezuka asks whether humanity is located in biological matter, perception, memory, love, or participation in the wider cycle of life and death.

## Cycles Instead of Escape

The series' recurring characters, reincarnating forms, and time-spanning structure resist a linear salvation narrative. The Phoenix does not simply rescue people from death. It reveals that the desire to escape death may itself trap the soul in suffering.

That makes the series a useful counterweight to Western immortality plots. Its answer is not permanent self-preservation but acceptance of recurrence, transformation, and responsibility within a larger cosmic rhythm.
