# The Marriage of Heaven and Hell

***The Marriage of Heaven and Hell*** is William Blake's illuminated work from the early 1790s, written as a visionary satire on conventional religion, moral dualism, and Swedenborgian order. It belongs in this graph because it deliberately scrambles the received labels of good and evil, treating energy, desire, and imaginative force as powers that official religion often misnames as hell.

## Hell as Energy

Blake's "Hell" is not simply the Christian Hell affirmed under another name. It is a polemical image for energy, desire, imagination, and creative force against a passive, restrictive, law-bound Heaven.

This places the work near [[Inverted Providence]], but not in the Sadean sense. Blake is not arguing that cruelty is the hidden law of reality. He is attacking moral and religious systems that call living energy evil because they fear freedom, body, imagination, and prophetic excess.

## Against Passive Goodness

The work repeatedly suggests that conventional virtue can become a form of weakness when it suppresses energy rather than ordering it toward real life. Blake's target is not goodness as such, but a moral vocabulary emptied into repression.

That makes the work useful beside [[Disordered Love]] in argument. Augustine's problem is desire loved in the wrong order; Blake's problem is desire falsely condemned by a dead order. The tension matters because the same vocabulary of liberation can either heal repression or become a permission for self-worship.

## Proverbs and Hidden Correspondences

The "Proverbs of Hell" treat reality as symbolic, aphoristic, and spiritually charged. Blake's images do not work as ordinary doctrine; they form a system of visionary correspondences where animals, bodies, angels, devils, flames, and books reveal states of soul.

This makes [[Hidden Correspondence System]] a strong bridge. Blake's cosmos is not flatly literal. Visible forms are read as spiritual energies and imaginative structures.

## Sexual Energy, Magic, and Revolution

The work helps explain why sexual liberation, magical imagination, and political revolution can become linked in this Romantic-esoteric branch. Repressed desire is not only private frustration; for Blake it belongs to the same deadening order that imprisons prophecy, body, and social life.

This connection should not be collapsed into [[Libertine Rationalism]]. Sade rationalizes domination and use. Blake treats desire as part of imaginative life against spiritual repression, though the graph should still ask whether such liberation can remain ordered toward love.

## Milton Reopened

Blake famously reads Milton as if the poet's imaginative energy exceeds his stated theology. That makes the work important beside *[[Paradise Lost]]*: Satanic rhetoric becomes available for Romantic reinterpretation because it can be received as energy against law.

*[[Prometheus Unbound]]* gives the liberating version of this move, while *[[Cain (Byron)]]* gives the wounded theological version. Blake gives the symbolic and aphoristic version.

## Why It Belongs Here

*The Marriage of Heaven and Hell* belongs because it clarifies a major ambiguity in the graph. The rebellion against dead order can expose real hollowness, but it can also invert moral language so completely that energy itself becomes the measure of truth.
