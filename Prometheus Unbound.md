# Prometheus Unbound

***Prometheus Unbound*** is Percy Bysshe Shelley's 1820 lyrical drama reworking the Prometheus myth into a vision of liberation, forgiveness, and cosmic renewal. It belongs in this graph because it gives one of the clearest Romantic alternatives to the Christian suspicion of rebellion: the rebel against heaven becomes the suffering liberator rather than the proud overreacher.

## Prometheus as Liberating Rebel

Shelley's Prometheus is not Victor Frankenstein and not Milton's Satan. He suffers under Jupiter's tyranny, but his victory comes through endurance and the renunciation of hatred rather than domination.

This makes the work important beside [[The Overreacher]] in argument. The graph usually treats overreach as desire outrunning wisdom, but Shelley offers a heroic counter-image: revolt against false authority as the path toward freedom.

## Jupiter and False Heaven

The drama depends on a vision of divine rule as tyranny. Jupiter's fall is therefore not rebellion against true goodness, but deliverance from a counterfeit order.

That places the work near *[[Paradise Lost]]* through Romantic reinterpretation. Where Milton's Satan turns liberty into pride, Shelley imagines rebellion purified of revenge and redirected toward universal reconciliation.

## Cosmic Renewal

After Jupiter's fall, the world is not merely politically improved. Nature, love, language, imagination, and human possibility are renewed together. The poem's revolution is cosmic and affective, not only institutional.

This connects the work to [[Progress as Secular Providence]] in a mythic form. History is not simply managed by policy or science; it is transfigured by the overthrow of false power and the release of love.

## Why It Belongs Here

*Prometheus Unbound* belongs because it clarifies the Romantic temptation and the Romantic hope. It shows why rebellion could become morally beautiful in modern literature, and therefore why later Christian writers such as Lewis and Tolkien are so careful about power, freedom, and creaturely limits.
