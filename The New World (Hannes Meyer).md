# The New World (Hannes Meyer)

***The New World*** (*Die neue Welt*, 1926) is Hannes Meyer's essay on the modern world of science, technology, mass culture, standardization, mobility, media, housing, and collective production. Written before Meyer became the second director of the Bauhaus, it helps explain the school's later social-functional turn: building is no longer primarily aesthetic composition, but a technical and social process.

It belongs in the graph because it makes the Bauhaus branch more explicit. The early Bauhaus manifesto still dreams in the language of craft, workshop, cathedral, and spiritual communal making. Meyer's essay pushes toward a world of function, economy, collective work, scientific mentality, housing need, and internationalized modern form.

Meyer later became Bauhaus director from 1928 to 1930. His communist commitments and social-functional emphasis made him controversial, and his place in later Bauhaus reception was often minimized or distorted.

The essay is one of the clearest cases of [[Art Into Life]] inside the Bauhaus orbit. Art is no longer chiefly a separate aesthetic object; it becomes part of a technical, social, and environmental reconstruction of modern life.

## The Mechanized Planet

Meyer opens from the sense that the entire world is being penetrated by scientific and mechanical order. Airships, planetariums, automobiles, photography, cinema, radio, electrical power, cables, signals, gasoline stations, dams, pylons, and industrial structures become the visible signs of a new environment.

This places the essay near [[Total Technical Environment]] in a non-fiction seed form. Meyer is not writing dystopia. He is describing modernity as a changed field of perception and habitation, where machines and media do not merely appear inside the world but help define what the world now feels like.

The essay is therefore useful beside later fictional technological environments by contrast. Meyer sees the technological environment as the basis for a new constructive order, while works such as *Metropolis*, *We*, and *BLAME!* reveal how such environments can become spiritually or politically destructive.

## Building as Technical Process

Meyer's most important architectural move is to subordinate building to function, economy, and life-process. The apartment becomes a kind of residence machine made from components of heating, light, hygiene, weather protection, cooking, radio, convenience, family life, and modern materials.

This is one of the clearest Bauhaus-adjacent cases of [[Machine for Living]]. Le Corbusier gives the slogan its famous architectural force, but Meyer gives it a more explicitly social and technical form. Housing is not a private aesthetic shell. It is a constructed answer to biological, domestic, economic, and technological needs.

The promise is again real: better housing, sanitation, light, efficiency, and material honesty. The danger is that dwelling can be reduced to an optimized process before it is understood as home, inheritance, memory, worship, neighborhood, and embodied relation.

The ADGB Trade Union School is the built case where Meyer's social-functional logic becomes especially clear. It is not only a school building, but a coordinated environment for union education, dwelling, health, movement, and collective discipline.

The Houses with Balcony Access give the domestic version of the same logic. Minimum dwelling, sunlight, cost, access, laundry, garden space, and neighborly encounter become parts of a planned social-housing system.

## Against Aesthetic Composition

Meyer attacks architecture as effect, tradition, and artistic composition. Forms should emerge from function, material, construction, and economy rather than from inherited style or individual artistic self-display.

This makes the essay a sharp contrast within [[Art Into Life]] and against [[Architecture as Public Language]]. Kandinsky seeks inner spiritual necessity through form. Ruskin seeks moral freedom in craft and imperfection. Meyer seeks an exact, collective, constructive form adequate to modern life.

The contrast should not be made too cheaply. Meyer is not merely vulgar utilitarianism. He is trying to free design from private taste and decorative privilege. But his solution risks making the full human meaning of building invisible when it cannot be translated into function.

## Collective Work for All

Meyer also redefines art and design as collective and socially directed. The artwork is not private luxury or collector's prize; it belongs to a world of shared need, exact form, and collective production.

This links the essay to the total-artwork question by contrast. Wagner imagines a total communal artwork through myth, drama, music, and festival. Meyer imagines collective modern work through construction, technology, function, and social usefulness.

That makes the essay important for the graph's art branch. The future artwork can become spiritual drama, revolutionary spectacle, industrial design, or total environment. Meyer pushes toward the designed environment for all.

## International Form and Stateless Construction

Meyer treats constructivist form as international rather than national. Modern materials, machines, standards, diagrams, and scientific thinking seem to produce forms that can travel beyond local tradition.

This is where the essay approaches [[High-Modernist Planning]], though it is not primarily a city-planning document. Its logic favors legibility, standardization, and reproducibility across borders. The danger is not only uniformity of appearance, but the weakening of local inherited forms as sources of judgment.

The same internationalism helps explain why Bauhaus ideas could become so influential. A chair, school, apartment, facade, housing estate, or city plan could be presented as modern because it answered function rather than place.

Moisei Ginzburg's position is the stronger Soviet Constructivist neighbor to Meyer's. Both reject private aestheticism in favor of modern social function, but Ginzburg places that shift inside a more explicitly revolutionary account of epoch, labor, machine, and new collective life.

Moholy-Nagy's *The New Vision* is the pedagogical and perceptual neighbor. Meyer emphasizes function, economy, and collective need; Moholy-Nagy emphasizes training the modern sensorium through material, light, photography, motion, and design.

## Why It Belongs Here

*The New World* belongs in the graph because it states the Bauhaus social-functional turn with unusual clarity. It shows modern architecture moving from craft and spiritual unity toward scientific environment, collective need, housing process, international form, and design as an instrument for modern life. It is one of the clearest texts for understanding how the built world becomes a field of technical and social reconstruction.
