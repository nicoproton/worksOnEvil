# High-Modernist Planning

High-Modernist Planning names the pattern in which a city, institution, region, or population is redesigned from above according to rational order, legibility, hygiene, circulation, efficiency, and social purpose. The planner sees the existing city as confused, unhealthy, obsolete, or morally failed, then proposes a simplified order that can be grasped, administered, and repeated.

This bridge belongs near [[Life Redesigned by Ideology]], [[Administered Human Formation]], [[Machine for Living]], [[Scientific World State]], and [[Total Technical Environment]]. It is useful when the built environment becomes a diagram of expert rule.

*[[Seeing Like a State]]* gives this bridge its clearest political-theoretical form. Scott's target is not planning by itself, but the combination of administrative simplification, high-modernist confidence, coercive power, and weakened local capacity to correct the plan.

*[[Cities of Tomorrow (Peter Hall)]]* gives the broader planning-history map. It helps distinguish Garden City decentralization, RPAA regionalism, Le Corbusier's functional city, highway planning, urban renewal, and state-market suburbia without losing the common modern wager that social life can be improved by spatial redesign.

Stein's *[[Toward New Towns for America]]* belongs on the softer edge of this bridge. It is not a Corbusian tower-city argument, but its superblocks, traffic separation, and planned neighborhood grammar show how humane community planning can still turn social life into a design problem solved before inhabitants arrive.

Watkin's *[[Morality and Architecture]]* helps explain why such plans can become resistant to correction. Through [[Moralized Architectural Determinism]], a design system can present itself as the morally necessary form of the age, so lived alienation is interpreted as public backwardness rather than as evidence against the plan.

Krier's *[[Architecture - Choice or Fate]]* gives the traditional-urbanist counterclaim. Functional zoning, megastructure, tower blocks, and suburban dispersion are not inevitable results of modern life; they are choices that can be judged against the older knowledge of streets, squares, quarters, mixed use, and human scale.

Curl's *[[Making Dystopia]]* gives the sharper architectural indictment of the same problem. It asks how modernist planning and public building could keep the language of common good while producing places experienced as alienating, over-scaled, placeless, or hostile to ordinary habitation.

Use this link when:

- streets, neighborhoods, or cities are replaced by abstract grids, axes, zones, towers, or superblocks;
- inherited local order is treated as disorder because it is hard to administer;
- circulation, hygiene, light, air, and efficiency become arguments for large-scale demolition or reconstruction;
- the plan looks clearer from above than from the pedestrian, household, market, parish, or neighborhood level;
- social improvement is expected to follow from spatial simplification.

*[[The City of Tomorrow and Its Planning]]* gives the bridge its Le Corbusier form. *[[Plan Voisin]]* is the sharpest project-case: old Paris is treated as material for a new rational order of towers, arteries, and green space. *[[The Radiant City]]* generalizes that impulse into a more portable doctrine of functional, green, administratively ordered urban life. *[[The Athens Charter]]* codifies the functional city, while [[Brasilia]] gives the graph a built capital where this planning grammar becomes political space.

*[[The Death and Life of Great American Cities]]* is the major counter-work. Jacobs does not deny that cities have order; she denies that their order can be understood chiefly from the aerial plan, the separated function, or the superblock. Her sidewalk, mixed-use, short-block, aged-building, and density arguments show a living order that high-modernist planning tends to flatten.

*[[A City is Not a Tree]]* gives the structural version of that critique. Alexander argues that living cities are overlapping semi-lattices, while artificial planned cities often become tree-like hierarchies whose clarity comes from suppressing overlap.

*[[A Pattern Language]]* gives Alexander's constructive alternative: not a master plan, but linked patterns that can guide piecemeal, user-participated, human-scale order.

*[[Seeing Like a State]]* should be used as the broader statecraft frame when the issue is not only the form of the city, but the administrative vision that makes forests, farms, names, measures, streets, and populations readable before they are governed.

This concept should remain distinct from [[Machine for Living]]. Machine for Living can apply to the house, apartment block, or city as functional apparatus. High-Modernist Planning emphasizes top-down simplification and administrative legibility at urban or social scale.
