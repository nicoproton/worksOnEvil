# A City is Not a Tree

***A City is Not a Tree*** is Christopher Alexander's 1965 essay arguing that living cities have overlapping structures more like a semi-lattice, while many planned modern cities are organized like trees: clean hierarchies in which each part belongs to one branch and overlaps are suppressed.

It belongs in the graph because it gives a structural account of the same problem Jane Jacobs sees at street level. *[[The Death and Life of Great American Cities]]* observes sidewalk life, mixed uses, and local complexity. Alexander gives the formal warning: the city dies when planners simplify overlapping life into separable units.

The essay's special value is narrow and powerful: it names the structural error by which artificial cities lose the overlapping relations found in living cities.

## Natural City and Artificial City

Alexander distinguishes natural cities from artificial cities. Natural cities are those that have grown through time, use, accident, repair, custom, and many overlapping decisions. Artificial cities are those deliberately made by planners or designers as complete schemes.

This gives the essay a direct relation to [[High-Modernist Planning]]. The problem is not that design exists. The problem is that planned design often produces a simplified structure because the designer must make the city legible before making it livable.

The artificial city tends to look clear because its parts are separated. But that clarity can be purchased by removing the very overlaps that make urban life rich.

## Tree and Semi-Lattice

The tree is a hierarchy. A part belongs to a branch, which belongs to a larger branch, and overlap between branches is limited. This is useful for administration, classification, and diagrams.

The semi-lattice allows overlapping sets. A school, shop, sidewalk, square, family route, work route, parish boundary, play area, and market life can share pieces of the same city without being reducible to one hierarchy.

This is why the essay is so important for the graph. It gives a formal way to say what [[Embodied Relation]] sees experientially: real life overlaps. Persons belong to many relations at once, and a city must leave room for those relations to cross.

## Against Functional Separation

Alexander's argument cuts into the same planning logic that appears in *[[The Athens Charter]]*. Functional planning wants dwelling, work, recreation, and circulation separated so each can be ordered clearly.

Alexander shows the cost of that separation. When every function receives its own branch, the city loses the overlapping fields where ordinary life actually happens.

This also sharpens the critique of *[[The Radiant City]]* and [[Brasilia]]. Alexander explicitly treats Brasilia as a tree-like artificial city: visually and administratively coherent, but impoverished in overlap at the lived level.

## Jacobs in Structural Language

Jacobs and Alexander are not the same thinker, but they belong close together in this graph. Jacobs gives the empirical and civic language: sidewalks, mixed uses, short blocks, old buildings, density, and public trust. Alexander gives the structural language: the living city is not a tree.

This relation should be kept as a strong thematic link rather than a claim that one simply derives from the other. Both resist the same modern simplification: the belief that the city's order must be clean before it can be good.

Together they form the main counter-current to the Le Corbusier-CIAM branch.

*[[A Pattern Language]]* is the constructive continuation of Alexander's critique. If this essay explains why tree-like planning damages living cities, the later book tries to describe patterns by which overlapping living order can be generated.

## The Danger of Design Intelligence

The essay is also useful because it criticizes a temptation in design intelligence itself. The planner's mind often seeks clarity, hierarchy, and manageable structure. But city life is not fully manageable in that way.

This connects the essay to [[Abstraction Without Prudence]]. The abstract model is not false because abstraction is useless. It is false when abstraction replaces practical judgment about how people actually live.

The danger is subtle: the planner may destroy overlap not out of malice, but because overlap is hard to draw, govern, budget, zone, and defend in a clean plan.

*[[Seeing Like a State]]* gives the broader statecraft version of the same danger. The tree-like city is one case of a larger administrative habit: making a living order readable by simplifying the overlaps that made it alive.

## Limits and Cautions

Alexander's semi-lattice language can itself become an abstraction if treated as a magic formula. The essay should not be used to imply that every old city is good, every planned intervention is bad, or every complex network is humane.

The point is sharper: living order requires overlapping memberships and relations. The planner must avoid replacing that complexity with a diagram whose cleanliness is mistaken for truth.

That makes the essay a corrective, not a total doctrine.

## Why It Belongs Here

*A City is Not a Tree* belongs in the graph because it explains why many modern planned environments feel lifeless even when they are rational, efficient, and visually ordered. It shows that the city is not merely a machine, diagram, or administrative hierarchy. It is a dense field of overlapping relations, and those overlaps are not noise. They are part of the city's life.
