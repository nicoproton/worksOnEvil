# Libertas

***Libertas*** is Pope Leo XIII's 1888 encyclical "On the Nature of Human Liberty." It belongs in this graph because it gives the Catholic account of why freedom is not the will's exemption from law, but the rational creature's power to pursue the good. Its central usefulness is the distinction between true liberty and [[Liberty Detached from Truth]].

This note uses the official English text published by the Vatican.

## Freedom Begins in Rational Nature

Leo begins by praising liberty as a high natural gift belonging to rational creatures. Human beings are not merely moved by instinct. Because they possess reason, they can deliberate, judge, choose, and become responsible for their actions.

This matters because the encyclical is not anti-freedom. Leo's argument depends on the dignity of freedom. The problem is not liberty itself, but the corruption of liberty when the will chooses under a false appearance of good.

That gives the note a strong link to [[Thomas Aquinas]]. Leo repeatedly uses the Thomistic account in which will follows intellect, freedom is ordered to reason, and the possibility of sin is not the perfection of liberty but its defect.

## Sin Is Not the Perfection of Liberty

The encyclical's most important move is metaphysical and moral: the ability to choose evil proves that a person has free will, but it does not make evil choice the perfection of freedom. Error is a defect of the intellect; sin is a defect in the use of liberty.

This is where *Libertas* becomes a major anchor for [[Liberty Detached from Truth]]. If freedom is imagined as pure choice, then every limit appears oppressive. If freedom is ordered to truth and good, then law, reason, grace, and virtue do not destroy freedom but help it reach its end.

In graph terms, this is a Catholic answer to many later confusions. False liberty often offers enlargement of the self; Leo argues that it actually diminishes the person by making the will serve error, passion, or another power.

## Law Protects Freedom

Leo then explains why law is necessary for liberty. Because human beings can mistake apparent goods for real goods, the will needs reason and law to guide it. Natural law is not arbitrary external force; it is reason's participation in eternal law, directing persons toward their proper end.

This links *Libertas* to [[Christian Civil Order]]. Civil law has value when it helps citizens live according to right reason and the common good. It becomes unjust when it commands what contradicts natural or divine law.

The encyclical therefore resists both anarchy and tyranny. Law does not become legitimate merely because the state commands it, but neither is liberty perfected by escaping all law.

## Public Liberty and Civil Society

Leo extends the argument from individuals to society. A community is not truly free when everyone does whatever he pleases. True public liberty means that civil law makes it easier for people to live according to reason, virtue, and the eternal law.

That makes *Libertas* a constructive companion to *[[Immortale Dei]]*. *Immortale Dei* gives the Christian constitution of civil society; *Libertas* explains the moral meaning of liberty inside that order.

The connection to [[Secular State Supremacy]] is the negative side. When political order treats will, majority, public opinion, or state autonomy as the source of right, liberty becomes a name for civil power detached from divine and natural law.

## Liberalism as Rationalism in Politics

One of Leo's sharper claims is that political liberalism carries rationalism into morality and public life. If autonomous human reason refuses submission to divine reason, then every man becomes a law to himself, and the collective reason of the community becomes the supreme guide of public affairs.

This is a precise political form of [[Weaponized Rationalism]]. Reason is not weaponized here through machines or scientific management, but through autonomy: reason makes itself source and judge of truth, then uses liberty to authorize that independence.

Leo argues that this road does not end in freedom. It can lead to moral disorder, majority tyranny, the public irrelevance of religion, and finally coercion by force when conscience and duty no longer bind.

## Modern Liberties Examined

The encyclical treats several modern liberties: worship, speech, press, teaching, conscience, and political participation. Leo's method is not simply to reject every use of these words. He asks whether the liberty is ordered to truth and good or whether it grants error and vice the standing of rights.

This gives the note a careful relation to [[Administered Human Formation]]. Leo especially worries about liberty of teaching when it means that any doctrine may form the minds of the young. Education is not neutral if it trains the mind away from truth.

But the note should not be reduced to censorship. Leo also preserves a legitimate field for inquiry, speech, and learning where God leaves matters to human discussion, and he praises true science and genuine learning when they remain within truth.

## Tolerance Is Not Approval

One of the most useful distinctions is Leo's account of toleration. Human law may tolerate some evils to prevent greater evils or preserve greater goods, just as divine providence permits evil without approving it.

This gives the node political subtlety. *Libertas* does not say every falsehood or evil must always be suppressed by civil power. It distinguishes right from toleration. Error has no moral right as error, but prudence may require that some evils be legally permitted for the common good.

This helps keep [[Christian Civil Order]] from becoming a simplistic fantasy of total coercive uniformity. A Christian civil order still needs prudence because law works within fallen conditions.

## Christian Liberty Against State Absolutism

Leo also gives a positive account of liberty of conscience in the Christian sense: the freedom to obey God's will without obstacle. This is why the Church can resist unjust commands and defend her own liberty against the state.

That point is important for the graph. The Catholic account is not servile obedience to any ruler. When civil authority commands what contradicts God, obedience becomes unlawful.

This gives *Libertas* a direct place beside [[Secular State Supremacy]]. The state becomes tyrannical not only when it oppresses bodies, but when it claims authority over conscience, worship, Church teaching, and moral truth.

## Why It Matters Here

*Libertas* sharpens a distinction that will matter across the vault:

- freedom is good because the person is rational and responsible;
- choice against reason and truth is not higher freedom but defective freedom;
- law can perfect liberty when it directs persons toward the good;
- civil society becomes disordered when majority will or state power replaces natural and divine law;
- tolerance may be prudent without becoming approval;
- the Church's liberty is part of true public liberty, not an exception to it.

Its best graph role is to prevent "freedom" from remaining an undefined good. Leo XIII gives a standard by which liberation can be tested: does it free the person for truth and goodness, or does it merely loosen the bonds that kept the will from serving error?

*[[Veritatis Splendor]]* later carries this standard into moral theology, especially conscience, concrete acts, and the refusal to make intention or consequence override moral truth.
