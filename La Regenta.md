# La Regenta

***La Regenta*** is Leopoldo Alas "Clarin"'s 1884-1885 novel about Ana Ozores, the young wife of an older former magistrate in the provincial city of Vetusta. It belongs in this graph as a Spanish realist cousin to *[[Madame Bovary]]*, with a stronger emphasis on religious hunger, social surveillance, and corrupt spiritual guidance.

## Vetusta and the Provincial Cage

Ana's life is shaped by a suffocating provincial world: gossip, church politics, status anxiety, sexual curiosity, and respectable hypocrisy. Vetusta is not merely a setting; it is a social atmosphere that watches, interprets, and traps.

This gives the novel a relation to [[Hollowed Order]]. Marriage, religion, civic respectability, and social rank remain outwardly intact, but the goods that should animate them are weakened by vanity, manipulation, and boredom.

## Romantic Hunger and Spiritual Hunger

Ana's dissatisfaction is not only erotic. She longs for intensity, purity, devotion, and a life that feels spiritually significant. That makes her more than a copy of Emma Bovary.

The novel belongs to [[Bovarysme]] because Ana's self-understanding is shaped by imagined forms of life she cannot reconcile with her actual marriage and city. But her borrowed life is partly religious and mystical, not only romantic or fashionable.

## Confession as Corrupting Guidance

Fermin de Pas, Ana's confessor, becomes one of the novel's most important figures because spiritual direction is entangled with ambition, possessiveness, and desire.

This places the novel near [[Corrupting Guide]]. Guidance is dangerous here because it appears under the sign of spiritual authority. Ana seeks direction, but the guide's own disorder helps turn direction into possession.

## Adultery and Social Exposure

Ana's relation with Don Alvaro Mesia gives the novel its Bovary-like adultery plot, but the consequences are shaped by Vetusta's whole social organism. Sin becomes spectacle, gossip, judgment, and revenge.

This connects the novel to [[Disordered Love]]. Ana's desire for love and significance is real, but it becomes destructive because it is drawn into vanity, seduction, possessive guidance, and social theatricality.

## Why It Belongs Here

*La Regenta* belongs because it deepens the *Madame Bovary* branch instead of merely repeating it. It shows Bovarysme under Catholic-provincial pressure, where romantic fantasy, spiritual aspiration, corrupt guidance, and social surveillance become inseparable.
