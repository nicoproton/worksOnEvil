# Privatio Boni

***Privatio boni*** is the theological and philosophical idea that evil is not an independent substance equal to good, but a deprivation, corruption, or absence of good. The idea is associated especially with Augustine and later Christian theology.

In literary analysis, the concept is useful when a villainous force cannot truly create. It can imitate, consume, invert, mechanize, or hollow out what already exists, but its power depends on parasitizing some prior good. This makes it especially relevant to stories of artificial life, false utopias, spiritual emptiness, and institutions that preserve the form of order after losing its substance.

This note should be used when the connection is ontological or moral: evil as lack, parody, corruption, or dependence.

Do not use this link merely because a work contains evil, darkness, monsters, apocalypse, corruption, or death. The link is strongest when the work itself, or a clearly stated critical reading of it, shows evil as derivative rather than creative: a missing due good, disordered love, imitation without true making, power that can only consume, or order hollowed out from within.

Use more precise bridge concepts when possible. [[Sterile Imitation]] applies when evil mimics life, creation, speech, or order without true fruitfulness. [[Hollowed Order]] applies when social, civic, familial, or institutional forms remain after their animating good has drained away. [[Disordered Love]] applies when evil appears as a real desire turned toward the wrong object, wrong measure, or wrong mode.

If a work presents evil as an active rival substance, cosmic principle, or opposed divine force, use [[Manichaeism and Zoroastrianism]] instead. If it presents the world as a prison maintained by false powers and salvation through hidden knowledge, use [[Gnosticism and its Critics]]. A work may still be read against [[Privatio Boni]], but the note should say so explicitly: privation is then an interpretive argument, not the work's native metaphysics.

The two strongest conceptual anchors are [[Augustine of Hippo]], who gives the vault its language of evil as privation and disordered love, and [[Thomas Aquinas]], who systematizes the idea through being, goodness, participation, and ordered ends.

Tolkien's mythology gives the concept a narrative form. In *[[The Silmarillion]]*, Melkor cannot originate being and so turns to discord, possession, and marring; in *[[The Lord of the Rings]]*, Sauron cannot create a living world of his own and instead organizes a [[Sterile Imitation]] of order.

The Mother series gives the concept an unusually intimate game form. Giygas in *[[EarthBound]]* expands as evil power while losing coherent selfhood, and Porky in *[[Mother 3]]* turns [[Disordered Love]] into absolute safety by becoming unreachable.
