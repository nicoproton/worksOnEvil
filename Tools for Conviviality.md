# Tools for Conviviality

***Tools for Conviviality*** is Ivan Illich's 1973 work on technology, tools, institutions, professional power, industrial society, and human autonomy. It belongs in this graph because Illich gives a way to criticize the machine without rejecting tools: the central question is whether a tool extends human agency in responsible relation or reorganizes life so that persons become dependent on the system.

Illich was a Catholic priest and social critic, but this work is not magisterial Catholic teaching. Its value here is institutional and anthropological. It gives a Catholic-adjacent critique of technical systems that overgrow the human scale they were meant to serve.

## Convivial Tools

Illich's positive term is conviviality. A convivial tool can be used by persons and communities without making them servants of an expert system. It extends action, creativity, cooperation, and local judgment.

This gives the book a strong relation to [[Subsidiarity]]. Illich is not simply asking whether a technology works. He asks whether it leaves room for smaller communities, ordinary persons, and local practices to remain agents rather than clients.

## The Threshold Where Tools Turn

The book's darker insight is that a good tool can cross a threshold. At first it increases capacity. Later it becomes so complex, compulsory, professionalized, or institutionally protected that it disables the competences it once served.

This makes the book a core anchor for [[Institutional Counterproductivity]]. School, medicine, transportation, and administration can all begin as helps and then become systems that manufacture dependence.

## Radical Monopoly

Illich's concept of [[Radical Monopoly]] is one of the book's strongest graph contributions. A tool can dominate not by eliminating competitors, but by reshaping the world so that access to a human good must pass through that tool.

This makes the book useful for the urban, education, medical, media, and technical branches at once. The question is not only who owns the system, but whether the system has remade the conditions under which ordinary life can be lived.

## Against the Machine as Social Destiny

The book belongs near [[Domination Through the Machine]], though with a different tone from Tolkien or Lewis. Illich is not describing demonic domination or mythic machinery. He is describing the social moment when tools cease to be aids and become systems that define what persons may need, know, do, or become.

It also touches [[Total Technical Environment]]. Industrial tools become most dangerous when they stop appearing as separate devices and become the normal environment in which action, movement, education, health, and decision are possible.

## Why It Belongs Here

*Tools for Conviviality* gives the graph a precise criterion for the technology branch. A tool is not corrupt because it is modern, complex, or powerful. It becomes spiritually and socially dangerous when it destroys the human capacities, local relations, limits, and forms of judgment that would let people use it freely.
