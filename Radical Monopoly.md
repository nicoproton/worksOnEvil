# Radical Monopoly

Radical Monopoly names Ivan Illich's pattern in which a tool, service, or product becomes so dominant that it reshapes the need it claims to satisfy. The monopoly is not only one company controlling a market. It is a whole technical or institutional form becoming the normal gateway to a human good.

This bridge belongs near [[Total Technical Environment]] because radical monopoly changes the world in which ordinary action happens. Once the environment has been remade around the dominant tool, people who do not use that tool are no longer simply choosing differently; they become practically excluded.

Use this link when:

- a tool or service becomes the compulsory route to a good it did not create;
- natural competence is made socially ineffective by institutional dominance;
- multiple providers still leave one dominant form in control of the need;
- the environment is redesigned so that refusal becomes exclusion;
- freedom of choice exists inside a narrowed field created by the tool itself.

*[[Tools for Conviviality]]* gives the general theory. Illich uses radical monopoly to explain how industrial tools can cross a threshold where they no longer extend human agency but reorganize society around themselves.

*[[Energy and Equity]]* gives the clearest transportation form. The car does not merely offer faster movement; above a certain threshold, it helps create a landscape in which those without cars lose access to ordinary participation.

*[[Deschooling Society]]* gives the educational form. Schooling can become the recognized gateway to learning, certification, and social legitimacy, making unschooled knowledge socially invisible.

*[[Medical Nemesis]]* gives the medical form. Professional medicine can become the recognized gateway to health, weakening non-institutional ways of bearing sickness, pain, aging, and death.

This concept helps keep the graph precise. A radical monopoly is not the same as a conspiracy or a tyrannical command. It can arise through convenience, law, infrastructure, credentialing, insurance, professional prestige, and ordinary dependence.
