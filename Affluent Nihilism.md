# Affluent Nihilism

Affluent Nihilism names the pattern in which material prosperity, consumption, sexual freedom, technique, media, and individual choice survive after older moral and metaphysical orders have been emptied out. It is not poverty, oppression, or overt revolutionary terror. It is the quieter condition in which comfort remains, but the ends of life become thin.

This bridge belongs near [[Progress as Secular Providence]] when the promised future no longer appears as heroic revolution, but as comfort, choice, entertainment, therapy, mobility, and prosperity without a shared account of truth or the good. It also belongs near [[Liberty Detached from Truth]], because the self is often left free in the reduced sense of being free to consume, desire, perform, or self-define.

Use this link when:

- liberation from inherited limits leaves a world of appetite, surfaces, status, or managed comfort;
- prosperity does not cure spiritual emptiness, but makes it more livable and harder to name;
- revolutionary negation survives after revolutionary hope has faded;
- persons become consumers, performers, bodies, data-points, or market actors without a higher end;
- the story's horror comes from the normality of emptiness rather than from open tyranny.

Augusto Del Noce's *[[The Age of Secularization]]* is the strongest philosophical anchor. Del Noce argues that Marxism can fail politically while its negations triumph culturally: once the messianic revolutionary element disappears, what remains can be a prosperous society without metaphysical truth, authority, or permanent values.

*[[The Crisis of Modernity]]* gives the more synthetic Del Noce form. The danger is not only communism, fascism, or liberal capitalism considered separately, but the modern drift in which scientism, relativism, progressivism, and the affluent society converge in practical nihilism.

*[[American Psycho]]* is a major fictional case. Patrick Bateman's world is prosperous, mobile, fashionable, and empty; violence does not rupture the surface-world so much as reveal what a world of surfaces can conceal.

*[[Cosmopolis]]* gives the financial-informational form. Wealth and abstraction do not enlarge Eric Packer's world; they seal him inside prediction, screens, risk, and bodily anxiety.

*[[White Noise]]* gives the domestic-consumer form. The family, supermarket, television, university, and pharmaceutical promise of relief all orbit a fear of death that consumer life can manage but not answer.

*[[Crash (Ballard)]]* gives the technological-desire form. The affluent technical environment does not merely transport bodies; it reprograms desire around injury, image, celebrity, and machine contact.

Walker Percy's *[[Lost in the Cosmos]]* and *[[Love in the Ruins]]* give Catholic comic forms. Prosperity, choice, therapy, and cultural scripts remain available, but the self and society become less able to say what they are for.

Josef Pieper's [[Leisure and Contemplation]] gives the positive counterpoint. A prosperous society may have free time, entertainment, and consumption while still lacking leisure in Pieper's sense: receptive openness to truth, beauty, worship, and being.

Jacques Maritain's [[Personalist Common Good]] gives another counterpoint. The problem is not only poverty or oppression, but a reduced image of the person: comfort and choice can remain while the person loses orientation toward truth, community, and final end.

This concept should not become a generic link for every consumer society. It belongs where the work notices that prosperity can coexist with, conceal, or intensify the collapse of a shared spiritual horizon.
