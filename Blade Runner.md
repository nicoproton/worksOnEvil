# Blade Runner

***Blade Runner*** is Ridley Scott's 1982 neo-noir science-fiction film, adapted from Philip K. Dick's *Do Androids Dream of Electric Sheep?* It follows Rick Deckard, a police officer assigned to hunt escaped bioengineered replicants in a decaying future Los Angeles. Its strongest contribution to the vault is the moral reversal of artificial life: the manufactured beings often appear more desperate, vulnerable, and spiritually alive than the humans who own or kill them.

## Replicants as Manufactured Persons

Replicants are synthetic humanoids created by the Tyrell Corporation for off-world labor. They are stronger than humans, legally disposable, and given deliberately shortened lifespans. Their rebellion begins with the demand for more life.

This makes the film a major node for [[Manufactured Humanity]] and [[Artificial Immortality]]. The replicants do not seek abstract domination; they seek an extension of lives intentionally engineered to expire.

## Tyrell's Pyramid

Tyrell's headquarters towers over the city like a temple, and Tyrell himself occupies the position of creator, father, and corporate god. The film's religious architecture matters: artificial life is produced by capital, but staged as a quasi-divine act.

This links the film to [[Machine as God]]. The sacred center of the city is not a church but a corporation that manufactures persons.

## Memory and Humanity

Rachael's implanted memories make the film's question more intimate. If memory can be manufactured, then identity becomes both fragile and morally charged. False memory does not make Rachael less capable of suffering; it makes her dependence on her maker more cruel.

This is where the film links to [[Simulated Reality]]. The false world is not a total virtual environment, as in *The Matrix*, but an interior simulation: a past that never happened, installed to stabilize an artificial person.

## Roy Batty's Demand

Roy Batty's confrontation with Tyrell is the film's theological center. A made being returns to his maker and asks for more life. Tyrell cannot grant it. The creator's power is exposed as technical and commercial, not salvific.

Roy's final mercy toward Deckard complicates the human/artificial boundary. The replicant marked for retirement becomes the one who preserves life, while the human hunter has spent the film administering death.

## Electric Sheep and the Source Novel

The film's relation to Philip K. Dick's novel is important but not identical. Dick's book places more emphasis on empathy, animals, religion, and the decay of authentic feeling. The film distills the question into image, atmosphere, memory, and lifespan.

For this vault, the film belongs beside *R.U.R.*, *Frankenstein*, and *Metropolis* because all four ask what creators owe to made beings who become morally legible as persons.

The source novel, *[[Do Androids Dream of Electric Sheep]]*, deserves a separate node because it centers Mercerism, empathy boxes, artificial animals, mood organs, and extinction guilt more strongly than the film.
