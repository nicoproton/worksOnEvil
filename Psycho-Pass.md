# Psycho-Pass

***Psycho-Pass*** is a cyberpunk anime series set in a future Japan governed by the Sibyl System, which scans citizens' mental states and predicts their likelihood of criminal behavior. Society appears peaceful because the system manages occupation, policing, and social risk. Its strongest graph connection is false salvation through quantified interiority.

## Crime Coefficients

The Sibyl System measures citizens' Psycho-Pass and Crime Coefficient, making inner life legible to state technology. People can be detained, treated, or executed not only for crimes committed, but for the potential to commit them.

This makes the series a strong case of [[Programmed Contentment]]. Social peace is produced by continuous psychological measurement and intervention.

## Dominators and Delegated Judgment

Police weapons called Dominators are authorized by Sibyl's judgment. Human agents enforce decisions whose moral logic has been outsourced to the system.

This places the series near [[Abstraction Without Prudence]]. Judgment becomes technical output, and ethical responsibility is displaced into an apparently objective apparatus.

## The Secret of Sibyl

Sibyl's hidden core is a collective of criminally asymptomatic brains: people unreadable by the system, incorporated into the very authority that judges everyone else.

This is a precise form of [[Respectable Front]]. The public believes in neutral computation, but the hidden system is a hive of exceptional human minds exercising concealed rule.

## Peace as Managed Personhood

Sibyl offers order, safety, and social function. It also narrows what a person may become. A cloudy Psycho-Pass becomes a civic danger; psychic irregularity is treated as pollution.

This makes the series central to [[Purification by Exclusion]] and [[Corrected Inwardness]]. The system saves society by defining acceptable human interiority, then correcting or removing what does not fit.

## Akane's Problem

Akane Tsunemori's importance is that she sees Sibyl's corruption without collapsing into simple nihilism. Her problem is not merely to expose the system, but to ask what justice can mean when society has become dependent on an unjust source of peace.

This gives the series a stronger philosophical profile than a simple surveillance dystopia. It asks whether a corrupt order can be resisted from within without becoming its instrument.
